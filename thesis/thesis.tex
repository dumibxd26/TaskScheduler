\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Scheduling a directed acyclic graph (DAG) on a heterogeneous cluster
couples several decisions: which ready task to dispatch, where to run
it, whether parent data should move, and whether urgent work justifies
interrupting running work. Optimising one response-time objective can
therefore worsen another, particularly when execution estimates are
learned online and preemption discards progress.

This thesis presents \emph{TaskScheduler}, an experimental workflow
scheduling platform with two complementary implementations. The first
is a deterministic discrete-event simulator with calibrated CPU,
memory/cache, storage, network, thermal, energy, failure/repair,
checkpoint, gang, release-time, and preemption models. It evaluates
FCFS, Random, EFT, HEFT, historical simulator Legacy, exact Legacy v1,
tuned exact Legacy, Adaptive, and Adaptive without preemption. The
second is a Kubernetes-native control plane built from
Workflow and TaskTemplate custom resources, a DAG controller, a custom
Pod scheduler, persistent online profiles, a producer-local HTTP data
plane, and node telemetry DaemonSets.

The simulation study covers five cluster families, fifteen workflow
families, four overlapping-workload traces, and disjoint exploratory
and confirmatory seeds. It retains 17,400 exploratory and 33,250
fresh-seed confirmatory matrix records. HEFT has the lowest
confirmatory closed-DAG mean regret (1.69\%) and P95 regret (5.75\%);
EFT is the close second. Under overlap, historical Legacy has the
highest deadline hit rate (60.00\%), Adaptive has the lowest P95
response regret (35.07\%), and EFT/HEFT have the lowest median energy.
A normalized 256-candidate search improves exact Legacy v1 on disjoint
holdout and confirmation, but tuned Legacy does not become the global
winner.

The live study executes 27 cyclically ordered trials across balanced,
homogeneous, and resource-skewed logical \texttt{kind} profiles on one
PC. All 135 workflows and 540 final task records succeed without task
failure, container restart, collection error, or scheduler error. Tuned
Legacy reduces aggregate v1 makespan by 3.50\% and is lower in all three
profiles. Relative to tuned Legacy, Adaptive reduces median workflow
response by 19.40\% but increases makespan by 14.80\%, slightly worsens
the P95, and performs 44 kill-and-restart preemptions.

The central result is consequently objective-specific rather than a
universal winner: HEFT/EFT are suitable static closed-DAG baselines,
historical simulator Legacy leads modelled deadline success, tuned
Legacy is preferred when the exact live scorer and batch completion are
primary, and Adaptive is preferable when median regular-workflow
response justifies restart cost. The \texttt{kind} measurements
establish integration behaviour on one physical host; physical
multi-machine validation remains future work.

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Kubernetes, workflow scheduling,
heterogeneous clusters, thermal-aware scheduling, bandwidth-aware
scheduling, UCB, ECT, CRDs, discrete-event simulation, kind.

\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:intro}

\section{Problem context}
A workflow is represented as a DAG $G=(V,E)$ whose vertices are tasks
and whose edges express execution or data dependencies. A task can run
only after its predecessors complete. Once ready, it still has to be
assigned to a node with compatible architecture and enough CPU and
memory. Its completion time depends on the selected node, co-running
tasks, image/startup delay, data location, network contention,
temperature, and failure history. This is a heterogeneous DAG
scheduling problem, for which even static formulations are generally
computationally difficult; production systems therefore rely on
heuristics such as HEFT~\cite{topcuoglu2002heft}.

Kubernetes exposes an appropriate execution substrate. Its scheduler
filters infeasible nodes, scores feasible nodes, and binds a Pod to a
selected node~\cite{kubernetes-scheduler}. The default scheduler is
general-purpose and intentionally does not know TaskScheduler's
workflow graph, learned per-template runtimes, producer-local output
locations, or experiment-specific risk model. Kubernetes also permits
a separate scheduler to claim Pods through \texttt{schedulerName}, so
domain-specific placement can be added without modifying Kubernetes.

The practical challenge is not simply adding more terms to a score.
A more aggressive policy may reduce the response of an urgent workflow
by preempting batch tasks, yet increase total completion time because
those tasks restart. Likewise, a locality preference can avoid a data
transfer but overload the producer node. The evaluation must therefore
report multiple objectives and avoid declaring a universal winner from
one aggregate metric.

\section{Research questions}
The work is organised around five research questions:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.6cm,style=nextline]
    \item[RQ1] Across controlled heterogeneous clusters and DAG
    families, which deployable heuristic gives the strongest
    closed-workflow makespan and regret behaviour?
    \item[RQ2] Under overlapping arrivals, how do deadline success,
    workflow response, total makespan, energy, and preemption trade off?
    \item[RQ3] Does seeded, out-of-sample weight optimization improve
    the exact Legacy scorer over its original vector, and which factors
    materially affect placement?
    \item[RQ4] Does the Kubernetes implementation execute the intended
    controller, placement, learning, locality, telemetry, and
    preemption paths under repeatable workloads and capacity profiles?
    \item[RQ5] Which conclusions agree across simulation and the live
    system, and which remain specific to one implementation or
    environment?
\end{description}

\section{Objectives}
The corresponding engineering objectives are:
\begin{enumerate}[label=O\arabic*., leftmargin=*]
    \item define validated schemas for heterogeneous clusters, DAGs,
    release times, priorities, gangs, checkpoints, and workload traces;
    \item implement deterministic resource and event models whose
    assumptions are explicit and independently testable;
    \item compare FCFS, Random, EFT, HEFT, three explicitly distinct
    Legacy variants, Adaptive, and an Adaptive no-preemption ablation
    over broad exploratory and untouched confirmatory matrices;
    \item parameterize the exact eight-factor Legacy scorer, tune it on
    disjoint training/validation cases, evaluate untouched holdout
    cases, and test factor ablations and controlled interventions;
    \item implement Workflow and TaskTemplate CRDs, a lifecycle
    controller, a custom scheduler, profile persistence, producer-local
    data movement, and node telemetry;
    \item execute cyclically balanced v1/tuned/Adaptive trials on three
    same-host \texttt{kind} capacity profiles and retain a
    machine-readable record of every workflow, task, placement,
    transfer, preemption, and scheduler error; and
    \item classify every conclusion by evidence source and state the
    physical-cluster validation that is still missing.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Contributions}
The project contributes five concrete artifacts and findings.
First, it provides a deterministic simulator that combines resource
interference, network sharing, thermal dynamics, failures,
checkpointing, gang scheduling, releases, and priority preemption in a
single event model. Second, it provides a Kubernetes-native execution
path with declarative workflow state and a separately deployable
scheduler. Third, it makes the live Legacy equation reproducible in
simulation, performs a normalized train/validation/holdout weight
search, and checks every factor by ablation or controlled activation.
Fourth, it contributes exploratory and fresh-seed confirmatory corpora
plus cyclic three-policy evidence across three logical \texttt{kind}
topologies. Fifth, it derives objective- and workflow-specific policy
recommendations while identifying where simulation and same-host live
execution support different scopes of claim.

This is an experimental systems contribution, not a proof that the
Adaptive heuristic is globally optimal. The simulator and live system
are separate implementations, the local thermal annotations are
synthetic, and the completed Kubernetes experiment uses one physical
host. These boundaries are part of the result rather than caveats to
be hidden.

\section{Thesis structure}
Chapter~\ref{ch:sota} positions the work against DAG and cluster
scheduling literature. Chapter~\ref{ch:model} defines the model and
implementation. Chapter~\ref{ch:method} describes the evidence design.
Chapter~\ref{ch:metrics} defines the reported metrics, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} presents simulation and \texttt{kind} results.
The final chapter gives objective-specific recommendations and the
remaining physical-validation protocol.

\chapter{State of the art}
\label{ch:sota}

\section{Heterogeneous DAG scheduling}
Let $\bar{w}_i$ be the average execution cost of task $i$ over
compatible processors and let $\bar{c}_{ij}$ be the average
communication cost of edge $(i,j)$. HEFT assigns each task the upward
rank
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{rank}_u(i)=\bar{w}_i+
\max_{j\in\operatorname{succ}(i)}
\left(\bar{c}_{ij}+\operatorname{rank}_u(j)\right),
\label{eq:heft-rank}
\end{equation}
with the maximum equal to zero for a sink. Tasks are considered in
non-increasing rank and placed on the processor that gives the earliest
finish time~\cite{topcuoglu2002heft}. The method is attractive because
it is inexpensive, communication-aware, and directly represents the
remaining critical path. It is therefore both a baseline in this work
and the source of the simulator's task-ordering rule.

HEFT is fundamentally a static list heuristic: it assumes a known DAG
and estimated computation/communication matrices. TaskScheduler adds
release times, concurrent workflows, resource contention, failures,
thermal state, gangs, and preemption. These additions do not preserve
HEFT's original semantics automatically. In particular, the live
controller exposes only ready Pods to the custom scheduler; the current
live scheduler does not reconstruct all future CRD edges. Consequently,
the simulator exercises full upward ranking, whereas the live Adaptive
trial primarily exercises priority, ECT, profile learning, locality,
risk terms, and preemption. This distinction prevents the common but
incorrect claim that both surfaces run ``the same HEFT policy.''

\section{Execution and interference models}
Two classical performance models motivate the simulator's calibrated
resource equations. Amdahl's law bounds parallel speedup by the serial
fraction of a task~\cite{amdahl1967}; TaskScheduler uses this relation
when converting requested cores into effective throughput. The
Roofline model bounds performance by the most restrictive computation
or bandwidth ceiling~\cite{williams2009roofline}. Analogously, the
simulator estimates isolated task time as the maximum of CPU, memory,
storage-read, and storage-write times rather than adding mutually
overlapped phases.

The implemented model extends those abstractions with shared-resource
allocation. CPU demand is capped by calibrated node throughput and
scaled for SMT and thermal throttling. DRAM bandwidth and shared cache
are divided among memory consumers. Storage rates use max-min allocation
subject to sequential/random device limits. Routed network flows use
progressive max-min filling over all links in their paths and a TCP
window ceiling. These are explicit modelling choices, not claims that
every real application overlaps resources perfectly or that a single
fluid allocator captures kernel-level scheduling.

\section{Locality, fairness, and cluster schedulers}
Data locality and fairness can conflict. Quincy models scheduling as a
flow problem to combine fair sharing with placement near input data
\cite{isard2009quincy}. Dominant Resource Fairness generalises
max-min fairness to users with heterogeneous multi-resource demands
\cite{ghodsi2011drf}. TaskScheduler does not reproduce either
algorithm. It uses producer-node metadata and estimated bytes to price
remote dependencies, while workflow priority and virtual runtime order
ready work. DRF remains useful context because CPU and memory
feasibility alone do not define fairness when task shapes differ.

Large production schedulers emphasise scalable state management as
much as the placement formula. Omega uses shared-state optimistic
concurrency to support multiple schedulers~\cite{schwarzkopf2013omega};
Borg combines admission, priority, resource reclamation, and long-lived
cluster management~\cite{verma2015borg}. Sparrow demonstrates a
decentralised low-latency design based on sampling rather than global
optimisation~\cite{ousterhout2013sparrow}. TaskScheduler is much
smaller: one custom scheduler maintains an in-memory model and binds
ready Pods. The comparison is therefore architectural, not a claim of
production-scale throughput.

\section{Online learning and exploration}
When runtime distributions are not known in advance, repeatedly
selecting the currently best observed node can lock a scheduler into an
early noisy estimate. Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) policies address the
exploration/exploitation tradeoff by adding an optimism term that
shrinks with the number of samples~\cite{auer2002ucb}. In a reward
maximisation convention, a representative form is
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{UCB}_i(t)=\bar{x}_i(t)+
\beta\sqrt{\frac{2\ln t}{n_i(t)}},
\label{eq:ucb}
\end{equation}
where $n_i(t)$ is the number of observations for arm $i$.

The live implementation uses costs rather than rewards. It first forces
coverage until every feasible node has at least three samples for a
task template. It then adjusts ECT using the optimistic side of the
profile estimate and continues learning from successful Pod
completions. Legacy also forces coverage and retains a 10\% random
exploration branch after coverage. The simulation Adaptive policy does
not learn UCB profiles during a run; it has direct access to scenario
parameters and adds fixed energy, transfer, thermal, and failure terms.
The shared name indicates a design family, not identical learning
semantics.

\section{Thermal awareness and preemption}
Temperature-aware placement can reduce hot spots and cooling demand.
Moore et al. showed that software placement informed by data-centre
thermal behaviour could substantially reduce cooling cost in their
modelled environment~\cite{moore2005cool}. TaskScheduler's simulator
uses a first-order resistance/capacitance model and a configured
throttling curve. The live policy instead consumes a node temperature
annotation and adds a fixed penalty when thermal headroom falls below
10\,$^\circ$C. The node agent first attempts Linux sysfs; when that
sensor is unavailable in the local container environment, it maps load
average to a synthetic value bounded between 40 and 85\,$^\circ$C.
Retained same-host annotations therefore validate signal plumbing and
score consumption, but cannot support a claim about real cooling or
thermal throttling.

Kubernetes priority preemption removes lower-priority Pods when doing
so makes a pending higher-priority Pod schedulable
\cite{kubernetes-preemption}. Victims may receive a graceful
termination period, and discarded work creates a delay before capacity
is usable. TaskScheduler's live planner applies stricter local budgets
but immediately deletes each victim and later restarts it; it does not migrate
memory state or checkpoint live Pods. The simulator separately supports
checkpointable tasks and remaining-work restoration. The two
mechanisms must not be conflated when interpreting results.

\section{Kubernetes extension mechanisms}
Custom resources extend the Kubernetes API, and pairing a custom
resource with a controller creates a declarative reconciliation API
\cite{kubernetes-crd}. TaskScheduler follows this pattern with Workflow
and TaskTemplate CRDs. A controller creates Pods only when dependencies
are satisfied and writes lifecycle state to the Workflow status
subresource. A separate scheduler watches unscheduled Pods whose
\texttt{schedulerName} is \texttt{ts-scheduler}, computes a placement,
and calls the binding subresource. Node-local fileservers, bandwidth
probes, and thermal collectors are DaemonSets, the standard mechanism
for one eligible Pod per node~\cite{kubernetes-daemonset}.

The live integration environment uses \texttt{kind}, which runs
Kubernetes nodes as containers on a local container runtime
\cite{kind-quickstart}. This makes cluster creation and image loading
repeatable, but all worker containers ultimately share one physical
machine. The thesis therefore treats \texttt{kind} as control-plane and
same-host systems evidence, not as a physical distributed-cluster
benchmark.

\section{Positioning of this work}
The project does not propose a new optimality theorem, a new bandit
bound, or a production-scale scheduler architecture. Its contribution
is a controlled comparison and a working vertical integration. It asks
what happens when DAG ordering, resource estimates, profile learning,
data locality, thermal/failure risk, gangs, and bounded preemption meet
in one testable system. The answer is empirical: the added mechanisms
produce specialists and tradeoffs rather than one dominant policy.

\chapter{Architecture and implementation}
\label{ch:model}

TaskScheduler has two implementations of the same scheduling problem:
a deterministic discrete-event simulator and a Kubernetes control
plane. They share validated domain models and policy names where the
intent is related, but they do not share a policy engine. This chapter
defines the common abstraction and then describes each implementation
separately.

\section{Problem abstraction}
A cluster is a graph $C=(N,L)$. Each node $n\in N$ has a node type,
logical and physical CPU capacity, memory/cache capacity, storage
limits, an idle/active power model, a thermal model, and an optional
failure process. Each directed link $\ell\in L$ has payload bandwidth,
latency, and loss parameters.

A workflow is a directed acyclic graph $G=(V,E)$. Task $i\in V$ has a
resource request, compatible node types, a workload class, an optional
release time and deadline, and optional checkpoint or gang metadata.
An edge $(i,j)\in E$ carries precedence and may carry $d_{ij}$ bytes of
data. A workload is a time-ordered collection of workflow submissions,
allowing policies to be evaluated under overlap rather than only on a
closed DAG.

A placement is feasible when compatibility, CPU, memory, dependency,
release, and gang constraints hold. No single scalar objective is
declared universally correct. The evaluation observes workflow
response, workload makespan, deadline success, energy, transfer,
failure, and preemption together. This is important because the
experiments show that minimising urgent response and minimising total
makespan can select different policies.

\section{Discrete-event scheduler}
The simulator advances to the next release, transfer completion,
resource completion, checkpoint, failure/repair, or thermal event.
Running tasks share CPU, memory bandwidth, cache, storage, and network
capacity. Network flows use progressive max-min filling over every link
in their route and a TCP-window ceiling. CPU throughput applies
calibrated per-core rates, Amdahl scaling, SMT efficiency, and thermal
throttling. Temperature follows a first-order resistance/capacitance
model. Failures interrupt resident tasks; checkpointable work resumes
from its latest saved resource state, while other work restarts.

EFT greedily chooses the feasible task/node placement with the smallest
estimated finish time. HEFT adds the upward-rank ordering of
Eq.~\ref{eq:heft-rank}. The historical simulator policy named
\texttt{legacy} starts from EFT and adds node-type specialisation and
small fragmentation terms. It predates the exact scorer adapter and is
retained only as an earlier-project baseline; it is not the live
eight-factor equation. The \texttt{legacy-v1} and
\texttt{legacy-tuned} simulator policies instead invoke that exact live
equation over deterministic simulator observations. Simulator Adaptive uses
HEFT ordering, places higher-priority ready tasks first, and minimises
\begin{equation}
S_{i,n}=\widehat{F}_{i,n}
 +0.002\widehat{E}_{i,n}
 +0.001\widehat{D}_{i,n}
 +10P^{\mathrm{therm}}_{i,n}
 +100P^{\mathrm{fail}}_{i,n},
\label{eq:sim-adaptive}
\end{equation}
where $\widehat{F}$ is estimated finish time in seconds,
$\widehat{E}$ is active energy in joules, and $\widehat{D}$ is remote
transfer in MiB. Thermal pressure increases as headroom falls within
20\,$^\circ$C of the throttle point. Failure probability is the
configured hourly rate scaled by estimated execution time and capped at
one. The Adaptive-no-preemption policy uses exactly this ordering and
score with priority preemption disabled, making it an explicit
ablation.

When a high-priority ready task cannot fit, simulator Adaptive may
interrupt lower-priority running work. Victim selection prefers a set
that releases sufficient resources while accounting for whether a
checkpoint exists. This is functional preemption: it changes event
state and either restores checkpointed remaining work or restarts the
victim.

\section{Kubernetes control plane}
The deployable system consists of two CRDs, two control-plane
Deployments, and three node agents (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}):

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Workflow and TaskTemplate CRDs.} A Workflow stores
    task references, DAG dependencies, priority, deadline, and status.
    Reusable TaskTemplates define images, commands, resource requests,
    compatibility, and output-size hints.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{ts-controller}.} The controller creates a
    Pod only after all of its parents finish, annotates it with task and
    producer-location metadata, and sets its \texttt{schedulerName} to
    \texttt{ts-scheduler}. It records outputs and status,
    retries interrupted tasks, and recursively fails descendants that
    can no longer become ready.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{ts-scheduler}.} The scheduler watches its
    Pending Pods, reconstructs model objects, invokes the selected live
    policy, applies any victim deletions, and binds placements. It also
    records startup/runtime/failure profiles and persists them.
    \item \textbf{Node agents.} The \texttt{ts-fileserver} exposes
    node-local output files. \texttt{ts-bw-probe} measures peer HTTP
    throughput. \texttt{ts-thermal} publishes a temperature signal.
    The latter two write Node annotations consumed by the scheduler.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Live ordering and ECT}
\label{sec:algo}
The live policy interface returns explicit place, hold, and preempt
actions. The static Kustomize manifest selects Adaptive; the
\texttt{TS\_POLICY} variable and experiment harness select explicit
policy names for comparison. Exact Legacy applies an eight-factor score
\begin{equation}
L_{i,n}=\sum_{k=1}^{8} w_k\phi_k(i,n),
\label{eq:legacy-score}
\end{equation}
where the normalized features represent node-type affinity, resource
fit, forecast availability, image warmth, load balance, observed
failure rate, parent-data locality, and memory pressure. Higher scores
are preferred. Both exact variants force three observations per
compatible node and later retain a 10\% random exploration probability;
only their weights differ.

exact live scorer.

Adaptive groups ready tasks by workflow and orders groups by descending
effective priority, ascending virtual runtime, and arrival time. It
then orders each group's ready tasks by stored upward rank. The current
Kubernetes adapter, however, constructs scheduler-side workflow
templates with an empty edge list. The controller enforces dependency
readiness, but future successors are absent from the scheduler model;
therefore the completed live experiment does not validate full HEFT
upward-rank scheduling.

For candidate task $i$ and node $n$, live Adaptive estimates
\begin{equation}
\widehat{F}_{i,n}=\max(t_{\mathrm{now}},o_n)
 +\widehat{s}_{i,n}
 +\sum_{p\in\operatorname{parents}(i),\,a_p\ne n}
   \frac{d_{pi}}{b_{a_p,n}}
 +\widehat{r}_{i,n},
\label{eq:ect}
\end{equation}
where $o_n$ is occupancy reserved earlier in the same scheduling tick,
$\widehat{s}$ and $\widehat{r}$ are median startup and runtime profiles,
$a_p$ is the producer node, and $b$ is measured or default bandwidth.
Cold pairs use 2\,s startup and 10\,s runtime defaults. Same-node inputs
have zero transfer cost.

Before exploiting this estimate, Adaptive schedules each feasible
task-template/node pair until the least-observed candidate has three
samples. It then applies an optimistic UCB cost adjustment and minimises
\begin{equation}
J_{i,n}=\widehat{F}_{i,n}
 +\min(0,U_{i,n}-\widehat{r}_{i,n})
 +50q_{i,n}
 +20\,\mathbb{1}[h_n<10],
\label{eq:J}
\end{equation}
where $U$ is the profile-store UCB cost, $q$ is observed failure rate,
and $h_n$ is thermal headroom in degrees Celsius. Gang placements are
committed all-or-none within a scheduling tick. If no node can fit a
non-gang task, the preemption planner may select strictly lower-priority
victims subject to per-node and per-task anti-thrashing budgets. The
live action is kill/restart: the scheduler deletes victims and the
controller recreates interrupted tasks.

\section{Live signals}
Two signals are collected by DaemonSets:
\begin{description}[style=nextline,leftmargin=2.8cm]
    \item[Inter-node bandwidth.] \texttt{ts-bw-probe} on each node
    fetches a probe object from every peer's \texttt{ts-fileserver},
    derives payload throughput, and writes per-peer bandwidth Node
    annotations consumed by the ECT estimator.
    \item[CPU temperature.] \texttt{ts-thermal} reads
    the available Linux thermal source and publishes temperature and
    headroom annotations. The live score uses this as a threshold
    signal; unlike the simulator, it does not evolve an RC model.
\end{description}

\section{Producer-local data plane}
Each node owns a host-path directory \texttt{/var/lib/ts-data} that
the controller mounts into every worker Pod at \texttt{/data/outputs}.
Same-node parents are read through that mount with no network
involvement. For cross-node parents, the controller injects
\texttt{TS\_PARENT\_<NAME>\_FILESERVER\_URL} so the task script can
\texttt{curl} the parent's output through the producer's
\texttt{ts-fileserver}. This is intentionally simple: no shared
filesystem, no object store --- the data plane and the bandwidth
signal share exactly the same path, so they stay consistent.

The controller propagates producer node and output-byte metadata to the
child Pod. The scheduler converts that metadata into
\texttt{input\_bytes\_by\_node}, allowing Eq.~\ref{eq:ect} to account
for remote bytes before binding. At execution time, same-node children
read the host path directly; cross-node children use the producer's
HTTP endpoint. Thus the locality score and observed transfer traverse
the same producer identity, although estimated and realised bandwidth
can still differ.

\chapter{Experimental methodology}
\label{ch:method}

\section{Implementation as the foundation}
A working implementation is treated as a prerequisite for any
quantitative claim. Two execution surfaces are maintained in parallel,
but they are not the same program and their outputs are not assumed to
be numerically interchangeable:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item A \textbf{discrete-event simulator}
    (\texttt{simulation/}) executes independent simulator policy
    implementations against explicit cluster, workflow, and workload
    schemas. Given the same configuration and seed, the event order and
    sampled uncertainty are reproducible. This surface supports
    controlled algorithm comparison, including failures, repair,
    checkpointing, thermal throttling, transfer contention, gangs, and
    priority preemption.
    \item A \textbf{Kubernetes control plane} executes real Pods through
    the Workflow controller and custom scheduler. Its Legacy and
    Adaptive policies are live implementations over Kubernetes state,
    learned profiles, and node annotations. They share selected data
    models with the simulator, but not an identical scheduling code
    path.
\end{enumerate}

The evidence is therefore tiered. Simulation results provide controlled
comparative evidence about policy behaviour under the stated model.
Same-host \texttt{kind} results establish control-plane correctness and
provide descriptive live performance evidence. Physical multi-machine
\texttt{k3s} validation is specified by repository scripts but was not
performed for this evaluation. The thesis does not infer physical
network, thermal, or failure behaviour from the \texttt{kind} trials.

\section{Legacy weight search and factor checks}
The Legacy study changes only the weights in
Eq.~\ref{eq:legacy-score}. Candidate signs are fixed---rewards remain
non-negative and penalties non-positive---and every vector is
normalized so that
\begin{equation}
\sum_{k=1}^{8}|w_k|=125.
\end{equation}
This removes scalar duplicates because multiplying all terms by one
positive constant cannot change a node ordering.

A seeded log-normal search generates 256 local-to-broad perturbations
of Legacy v1, including the baseline itself. Ninety training cases
cover five clusters, nine workflow families, and seeds 0--1. The best
25 training candidates plus the baseline advance to 45 validation
cases formed from three different workflow families and seeds 17, 29,
and 43. Selection minimizes the fixed objective
\begin{align}
J_w={}&0.45\,\overline{r}_{M}
 +0.20\,r_{M,95}
 +0.15\,\overline{r}_{T}
 +0.10\,\overline{r}_{E} \nonumber\\
 &+0.05\,\overline{r}_{D}
 +0.05\,r_{L},
\label{eq:weight-objective}
\end{align}
where ratios compare candidate metrics with Legacy v1 for aligned
cases. A candidate is eligible only if validation objective, deadline
misses, and retries are no worse than v1 and its P95 makespan ratio is
at most 1.05. The selected vector is then evaluated, without further
selection, on 75 holdout cases using three unseen workflow families
and seeds 101, 103, 107, 109, and 113. Both empty and independently
prewarmed profile stores are reported; warm stores contain twelve
seeded observations per compatible task/node pair.

Factor evidence uses a second fresh block. Seeds 300--309 cross all
five clusters and fifteen workflows, producing 750 cases for Legacy
v1, tuned Legacy, and each scale-renormalized leave-one-factor-out
variant. Because broad corpora need not activate every term, separate
two-node interventions test whether warm image, observed failure, and
memory pressure can each reverse a placement while the other score
inputs are controlled.

\section{Simulation corpora}
The closed-workflow matrix crosses five cluster families with fifteen
workflow families. Cluster scenarios cover an asymmetric five-node
edge network, a balanced heterogeneous four-node cluster, a homogeneous
four-node cluster, a locally calibrated host model, and an unreliable
thermal three-node cluster. Workflow scenarios cover cache-sensitive,
checkpoint/recovery, CPU-heavy, deadline-critical, data-heavy, deep,
diamond, fan-in, fan-out, gang, I/O-heavy, linear, memory-heavy, mixed,
and wide DAGs.

The exploratory phase evaluates every cluster/workflow pair under
seeds 0--19 with FCFS, Random, EFT, HEFT, historical Legacy, exact
Legacy v1, tuned exact Legacy, Adaptive without preemption, and
Adaptive. For every seed, declared CPU, memory, storage, network, and
power uncertainty is sampled deterministically from a digest of
cluster identifier and seed. The resulting corpus has
\begin{equation}
5\text{ clusters}\times15\text{ workflows}\times20\text{ seeds}
\times9\text{ policies}=13{,}500
\end{equation}
deployable-policy records. Exhaustive static assignment is additionally
evaluated where the assignment space is tractable, adding 300 reference
records and bringing the saved exploratory total to 13,800.

The overlapping-workload matrix crosses the same five clusters with
four arrival traces: data-locality wave, mixed arrivals, priority burst,
and recovery/gang mix. Each trace contains multiple workflow
submissions with explicit release offsets; selected submissions also
have deadlines. With the same nine policies and twenty uncertainty
seeds, this corpus contains
\begin{equation}
5\times4\times20\times9=3{,}600
\end{equation}
records over 400 comparable cluster/trace/seed cases.

Confirmation uses untouched seeds 200--249 and the seven serious
common policies: EFT, HEFT, all three Legacy variants, Adaptive without
preemption, and Adaptive. FCFS and Random remain in exploration as
controls but are not rerun after their broad inferiority is established.
The closed confirmatory matrix therefore contains
$5\times15\times50\times7=26{,}250$ rows over 3,750 cases; the
overlapping matrix contains $5\times4\times50\times7=7{,}000$ rows over
1,000 cases. Exploratory and confirmatory summaries remain separate so
the latter is not silently pooled into model selection.

\section{Live workload and protocol}
The final live harness compares exact Legacy v1, tuned exact Legacy,
and Adaptive. Each trial first submits a
six-task best-effort CPU pressure workflow. Seven seconds later it
submits a non-preemptable two-task critical workflow, followed by a
three-stage data pipeline, a one-to-four fan-out, and four independent
heterogeneous tasks. Thus every trial has five workflows and twenty
final task records.

Each policy is run three times on each topology. Cyclic order gives
every policy each execution position once: v1/tuned/Adaptive,
tuned/Adaptive/v1, and Adaptive/v1/tuned. Before each trial the harness
deletes prior experiment objects, deploys the requested policy, and
verifies policy identity from logs. A profile path is isolated by
policy and topology run but shared across that policy's three
repetitions, so later repetitions can use earlier observations without
cross-policy contamination. Exact Legacy weights are copied into the
artifact. The harness waits for all five workflows, records CRD, Pod,
transfer, placement, preemption, and scheduler-log evidence, and pauses
six seconds before the next trial. These are sequential same-machine
repetitions with evolving profiles, not IID samples; live summaries are
descriptive and have no population confidence interval. Per-repetition
makespan and response are retained to test whether later profile states
coincide with improvement, but cyclic execution position, image/cache
state, and shared-host noise prevent attributing a trend to learning
alone.

\section{Derived comparisons}
For a closed case $c=(\text{cluster},\text{workflow},\text{seed})$,
policy regret is
\begin{equation}
R_{p,c}=\frac{M_{p,c}}{\min_{q\in Q_c}M_{q,c}}-1,
\label{eq:regret}
\end{equation}
where $Q_c$ is the set of deployable policies common to that
comparison. A policy ``win'' means
$R_{p,c}\leq10^{-9}$, so exact simulator ties count for every tied
policy. Mean-regret intervals are percentile bootstrap intervals from
2,000 deterministic resamples (bootstrap seed 2026). The static oracle
is excluded from this denominator and reported separately as a gap on
the 300 exploratory cases where it exists. This makes exploratory and
confirmatory regret directly comparable. Intervals describe variation
across the designed corpus; they do not imply that scenarios are a
random sample of all deployments.

An independent optimistic lower bound is the maximum of (i) a critical
path formed from each task's fastest compatible isolated execution and
(ii) aggregate CPU, memory-bandwidth, storage-read, and storage-write
work divided by total corresponding cluster capacity. The bound ignores
contention, communication, cold starts, failures, and placement
compatibility interactions, so a ratio above one is expected. The
static oracle enumerates node assignments but still executes them
through the simulator; it is a small-case reference, not a global
lower bound.

For overlapping case $c=(\text{cluster},\text{trace},\text{seed})$,
the analogous regret uses mean per-submission response instead of
makespan. Ranking sorts median response regret, then 95th-percentile
response regret, then policy name for deterministic ties. Deadline hit
rate, workload makespan, energy, and preemption count remain co-equal
reported outcomes rather than being folded into that rank.

\section{Reproducibility and evidence artifacts}
All scenario inputs are versioned under \path{scenarios/}. The
\path{run_experiment_lab.py} CLI selects scenario sets, policy
sets, half-open seed ranges, uncertainty sampling, oracle limits, and
output directories. It writes raw JSON Lines and CSV before deriving
JSON and Markdown summaries. The all-version exploratory corpora are
retained under \path{results/final-all-legacy-20seed/} and
\path{results/final-all-workload-20seed/}. Fresh-seed rows are under
\path{results/confirmatory-single-seeds200-249/} and
\path{results/confirmatory-workload-seeds200-249/}. The deterministic
\path{analyze_final_evidence.py} synthesis is generated from those raw
JSON Lines files as \path{results/final-evidence-synthesis.json} and
its Markdown companion. Weight-search, ablation, and controlled-intervention
artifacts are retained separately so aggregate policy comparison cannot
be confused with parameter selection.

The live harness is \path{run_k8s_experiment.py};
\path{scripts/run-kind-topology-matrix.ps1} recreates and validates all
three profiles. One JSON document per topology retains cluster metadata,
timestamps, exact weights, every workflow and final task record,
scheduler events, and aggregate summaries under
\path{results/k8s-kind-topologies/}. The earlier balanced
Legacy/Adaptive pilot remains under \path{results/k8s-kind/} as
formative evidence rather than being merged into the three-policy
matrix. Physical \texttt{k3s} scripts remain an unexecuted validation
path and are not counted as experimental evidence.

\section{Threats to validity}
The simulator is a model, not a calibrated digital twin of every
target. Its fluid sharing, overlap assumptions, uncertainty ranges, and
scenario deadlines can influence policy order. The broad factorial
matrix reduces dependence on one topology but does not remove model
risk. Exact ties are common because several policies choose the same
placement on simple ready frontiers. The selected Legacy vector is the
best eligible validation candidate among 256 samples for one fixed
objective; it is not a global optimum, and the holdout does not cover
every possible workload distribution.

The live experiment executes real reconciliation, binding, deletion,
retry, data movement, and containers, but all logical workers share one
Windows/Docker host. Container caps create visible heterogeneity without
creating independent hardware, network links, or thermal domains.
Injected temperatures validate metadata consumption only. Three
sequential repetitions per policy and topology can reveal repeatable
mechanisms and directional agreement, but not estimate population-level
performance. Profile evolution and shared-host background load remain
possible order effects despite cyclic balancing. Physical multi-machine
validation is therefore the principal external-validity gap.

\chapter{Contributions}
\label{ch:contrib}

\section{Scheduling and simulation contributions}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item A schema-driven deterministic discrete-event simulator for
    heterogeneous DAG workloads. It combines explicit CPU, cache,
    memory, storage, routed-network, thermal, power, failure/repair,
    checkpoint, release-time, gang, and priority-preemption behaviour
    in one executable model.
    \item Nine comparable simulator policies: FCFS and seeded Random
    controls; EFT and full-DAG HEFT baselines; historical simulator
    Legacy; exact Legacy v1 and tuned Legacy; and Adaptive with and
    without preemption. The Adaptive pair changes only interruption,
    while the exact Legacy pair changes only weights.
    \item A workload compiler that preserves overlapping submission
    times and returns per-submission response, deadline, and lateness
    metrics from one shared simulation run.
    \item Independent optimistic lower bounds and an exhaustive static
    assignment reference for tractable DAGs. These anchors distinguish
    relative policy wins from absolute distance to an idealised bound.
    \item A seeded, scale-normalized Legacy weight search with disjoint
    training, validation, and holdout workflows; deterministic warm
    profiles; fresh-seed leave-one-factor-out evaluation; and controlled
    placement reversals for factors inactive in the broad corpus.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Kubernetes engineering contributions}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item A Kubernetes-native vertical path comprising Workflow and
    TaskTemplate CRDs, a DAG controller, a custom scheduler, profile
    persistence, RBAC, and fileserver, bandwidth, and thermal
    DaemonSets. Root Kustomize manifests provide one installation entry
    point.
    \item A precise ownership split: the controller owns dependency
    progression and terminal-state propagation, while the scheduler
    owns ready-Pod ordering, placement, binding, learning, and victim
    selection. Interrupted Pods return through the controller's retry
    path; descendants of failed tasks terminate recursively rather than
    waiting forever.
    \item A producer-local data contract that carries node identity and
    output byte counts from completed parents into scheduling and task
    execution. The same metadata drives ECT transfer cost, direct local
    reads, and remote HTTP fetches.
    \item A live Adaptive policy that joins priority and virtual-runtime
    ordering with occupancy-aware ECT, coverage-first profile learning,
    an optimistic UCB adjustment, failure history, thermal headroom,
    gang rollback, and bounded kill/restart preemption.
    \item A repeatable live experiment harness that cyclically balances
    three policies, isolates profiles by policy/topology, verifies
    scheduler identity and exact weights, cleans resources, and retains
    workflow, task, transfer, placement, preemption, restart, and error
    evidence. A matrix runner recreates balanced, homogeneous, and
    resource-skewed \texttt{kind} clusters.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Empirical contributions}
The saved corpora provide four complementary findings. First, the
13,800 exploratory and 26,250 untouched-seed confirmatory closed-DAG
records show that informed list schedulers have family-specific tails;
HEFT has the lowest mean and P95 regret, while no policy dominates every
cluster/workflow family. Second, 3,600 exploratory and 7,000
confirmatory overlapping-workload records expose deadline, response,
energy, and makespan tradeoffs and isolate simulator preemption. Third,
the Legacy search, broad ablation, and controlled factor interventions
separate weight quality from factor activation. Finally, retained live
\texttt{kind} trials exercise binding, persistent learning,
producer-local data, and real kill/restart actions. This mechanism-level
account is more useful than declaring a single winner.

\section{Explicit non-contributions}
This work does not prove scheduler or weight optimality, establish a new UCB regret bound,
or claim production-scale scheduler throughput. Simulator and live
policies are related implementations rather than shared code. The live
trials do not exercise full-DAG HEFT ranking, real thermal throttling,
independent network links, or physical node failures. Multi-architecture
build and \texttt{k3s} deployment scripts exist, but successful
multi-machine execution is not evidence produced by this evaluation.

\chapter{Evaluation metrics}
\label{ch:metrics}

Metrics are defined against the clocks and records of each execution
surface. Simulator seconds are virtual time under a sampled scenario;
live seconds are differences between UTC Kubernetes/controller
timestamps. They are compared qualitatively across surfaces but are not
pooled into one statistical sample.

\section{Closed-workflow simulation metrics}
For a single workflow released at zero, \emph{makespan} $M_{p,c}$ is
the virtual time at which all tasks reach a terminal state. The primary
relative measures are regret $R_{p,c}$ from Eq.~\ref{eq:regret}, win
rate, and the ratio $M_{p,c}/L_c$ to the optimistic lower bound. Regret
normalises large and small scenarios while the bound ratio preserves an
absolute optimality reference independent of the policy set.
On the tractable exploratory subset, a second table reports each
deployable policy's makespan gap to exhaustive static assignment. The
oracle is not mixed into the deployable regret denominator.

The closed record also retains total node energy in joules, routed
network transfer in MiB, cold starts, task retries, node failures,
maximum node temperature, deadline success, and positive lateness.
Deadline hit rate is computed only over workflows with a configured
deadline. A policy can therefore have good makespan regret but still be
undesirable on energy, transfer, reliability, or thermal grounds.

\section{Overlapping-workload metrics}
For submission $w$ with release time $a_w$ and terminal time $f_w$,
response is
\begin{equation}
T_w=f_w-a_w.
\end{equation}
Each cluster/trace/policy/seed record stores mean and nearest-rank
95th-percentile submission response. Workload makespan is the virtual
span from the first release to the final terminal event. Response
regret uses the minimum mean response among recorded policies for the
same cluster, trace, and seed.

Deadline hit rate is the fraction of deadline-bearing submissions that
finish by $a_w+D_w$. The record additionally stores deadline misses,
task preemptions, retries, node failures, total energy, and network
transfer. Adaptive's preemption count is interpreted together with its
no-preemption ablation; count alone does not indicate whether an
interruption was beneficial.

\section{Live Kubernetes metrics}
Live workflow response is the interval from Workflow-object creation to
its recorded finish timestamp. A trial's makespan spans the earliest of
its five creation timestamps to the latest finish. The critical-response
metric applies the same definition to the delayed critical workflow.
Per-topology policy summaries report the median over 15 workflow
responses, their nearest-rank 95th percentile, the median of three
trial makespans, and the median critical response over three critical
workflows. Aggregate summaries apply the same definitions to all nine
trials and 45 workflows for a policy rather than averaging topology
medians.

Task evidence includes assigned node, final phase/state, startup,
container and status runtimes, output bytes, measured transfer seconds,
and local/remote transfer mode. Operational counters include successful
workflows, final task records, scheduler binding lines, kill/restart
preemption lines, container restart counts, failed task states, and
scheduler error lines. These counters are essential validity checks:
short response obtained by losing work would not be a successful result.

\section{Aggregation and reporting}
Medians and nearest-rank 95th percentiles are preferred for skewed
regret and response distributions. Closed-policy tables additionally
report mean regret and its deterministic 2,000-resample percentile
interval because means expose severe tail costs hidden by the many exact
ties. Percent differences in the live comparison use Legacy as the
reference only when reproducing the earlier pilot. General pairwise
comparisons use policy $b$ as the declared baseline:
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{p/b}=100\frac{x_p-x_b}{x_b}.
\end{equation}
Negative values are improvements only for duration metrics. Energy,
preemption, and completion are reported directly rather than forced
into that convention.

Jain fairness, continuous thermal exposure, per-task isolated slowdown,
and Pending-to-Scheduled latency were considered during design but are
not present in the definitive result artifacts. They are not used to
support the conclusions below.

history changes \texttt{risky} to \texttt{healthy}; and low free memory
changes \texttt{pressured} to \texttt{safe}. Setting only the named
factor to zero reverses each choice. The broad tie therefore measures
factor prevalence in these scenarios, while the controlled cases
establish causal ability to affect placement.

insufficient. FCFS ties a recorded best in 61.33\% of exploratory cases
yet has 128.77\% mean and 691.00\% P95 regret. Random has 347.97\% mean
and 1673.04\% P95 regret. The informed policies are therefore not
merely benefiting from abundant capacity; placement information matters
in the hard tail.

The exhaustive static assignment is excluded from deployable-policy
regret and reported separately on its 300 tractable exploratory cases.

the sole mean-regret leader for cache-sensitive DAGs. Adaptive and its
no-preemption ablation lead fan-out and mixed families and share the
diamond lead with tuned Legacy. EFT and HEFT lead CPU-heavy DAGs;
EFT, HEFT, and historical Legacy tie across fan-in, I/O-heavy, and
memory-heavy families. Linear, deep, data-heavy, failure-recovery, and
gang families contain still broader ties. Policy selection should
therefore use workflow and topology knowledge rather than only the
global rank.

\section{Cross-surface interpretation}
The two surfaces agree on three qualitative points. First, informed
placement is far safer than Random in heterogeneous scenarios. Second,
no policy dominates all objectives and families. Third, prioritising
urgent response through preemption can transfer delay to lower-priority
work. They also agree on the tuned-v1 direction: tuned Legacy improves
v1 in paired simulation, and lowers live makespan in all three logical
profiles.

They do not agree numerically, nor should they. In overlapping
simulation, Adaptive's median makespan is 11.78\% below tuned Legacy;
in live \texttt{kind}, it is 14.80\% above tuned Legacy. Simulator
Adaptive has full DAG knowledge, fixed scenario parameters, and no UCB
learning, while live Adaptive sees the ready frontier, learns profiles,
and restarts deleted Pods. All 44 live victims are pressure tasks, and
the workers contend on one physical host. The reversal is therefore a
boundary result: simulation predicts policy tendencies under its model,
while \texttt{kind} reveals control-plane, interference, and restart
costs. It is not a failed attempt at cycle-accurate validation.

\section{Answers to the research questions}
\begin{description}[style=nextline,leftmargin=2.8cm]
    \item[RQ1.] For static closed DAGs, HEFT has the lowest mean
    makespan regret (1.69\%) and P95 regret (5.75\%); EFT is the close
    second. Family results still require specialists, so there is no
    universal winner.
    \item[RQ2.] Under overlap, historical Legacy gives the best
    deadline hit rate (60.00\%), Adaptive gives the best P95 response
    regret (35.07\%), four policies share the lowest median makespan
    (55.070\,s), and EFT/HEFT use the least median energy. Adaptive
    preemption improves the response tail but not median makespan over
    its ablation.
    \item[RQ3.] Seeded optimization improves exact Legacy v1. In
    confirmation, tuned/v1 paired mean ratios are 0.9714 for closed
    makespan and 0.9370 for overlap response, with intervals wholly
    below one. Broad ablation identifies type affinity, locality, and
    load balance as consequential in this corpus; controlled cases
    verify that infrequent warm-image, failure, and memory factors are
    still active.
    \item[RQ4.] Across three recreated capacity profiles, the live stack
    completes all 135 workflows and 540 final tasks, verifies exact
    weights and policy-isolated profiles, performs 44 real victim
    deletions/retries, and records 17 remote fetches plus ten local
    reads without task failure, restart, collection error, or scheduler
    error.
    \item[RQ5.] Simulation and live evidence agree on objective
    tradeoffs, the qualitative role of preemption, and aggregate
    tuned-v1 improvement. Live kill/restart overhead reverses the
    Adaptive-versus-tuned makespan ordering. Cross-surface validation is
    therefore mechanism-aware, not numerical equivalence.
\end{description}

\chapter{Conclusion and further work}
\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Summary}
TaskScheduler answers a practical question rather than proving a
universal scheduler: how should heterogeneous DAG placement be studied
when task criticality, locality, contention, online uncertainty,
thermal/failure risk, deadlines, and preemption interact?

The project contributes two working surfaces. A deterministic
discrete-event simulator supports nine policies and explicit resource,
network, thermal, energy, failure, checkpoint, gang, release, and
preemption behaviour. A Kubernetes-native implementation supplies two
CRDs, a dependency controller, exact Legacy v1, tuned Legacy, and
Adaptive policies,
persistent profiles, producer-local data, telemetry, binding, and
kill/restart preemption. Their semantic boundary is documented rather
than hidden: only the simulator has the complete DAG and its Adaptive
policy does not perform live UCB learning.

The 17,400 exploratory and 33,250 fresh-seed confirmatory matrix records
reject a single-winner conclusion. For closed DAGs, HEFT has the lowest
confirmatory mean regret (1.69\%) and P95 regret (5.75\%); EFT is a
close second. Under overlap, historical Legacy has the highest deadline
hit rate (60.00\%), Adaptive has the lowest P95 response regret
(35.07\%), four policies share the lowest median makespan
(55.070\,s), and EFT/HEFT have the lowest median energy. Tuned exact
Legacy improves exact v1 pairwise on both surfaces but does not become
the global winner.

The 27 same-host \texttt{kind} trials validate the complete control path
across three logical capacity profiles. All 135 workflows and 540 final
tasks succeed. Tuned Legacy lowers aggregate v1 makespan by 3.50\% and
does so in all three profiles. Adaptive lowers median workflow response
by 19.40\% relative to tuned Legacy, but raises makespan by 14.80\% and
issues 44 kill/restart preemptions; both exact Legacy variants issue
none. Seventeen remote fetches and ten local reads validate both data
paths. These are descriptive integration results on one physical host,
not physical multi-machine validation.

\section{Policy recommendation}
The appropriate policy follows the deployment objective:
\begin{description}[style=nextline,leftmargin=2.8cm]
    \item[Static, known DAGs.] Use HEFT as the default when full graph
    information is available. EFT is nearly equivalent in the global
    closed ranking and is simpler when critical-path ordering adds
    little.
    \item[Energy-sensitive overlap.] Prefer EFT (or HEFT when rank is
    useful). Both have 17.60\% lower median simulated energy than Legacy
    and Adaptive while sharing the best median workload makespan.
    \item[Deadline and batch completion.] Prefer Legacy when deadline
    hit rate and avoiding discarded work dominate, but preserve the
    identity distinction: the deadline leader is historical simulator
    Legacy and is not the live eight-factor scorer. When exact live
    Legacy semantics are required, use tuned Legacy rather than v1; it
    improves v1 in both fresh-seed matrices and lowers live makespan in
    all three logical profiles.
    \item[Regular workflow response.] Use Adaptive when reducing median,
    fan-out, pipeline, and parallel-workflow response justifies
    kill/restart cost. Tuned Legacy is slightly better on aggregate
    critical response and substantially better on pressure work, while
    Adaptive has a worse P95 and total makespan. Preemption budgets must
    therefore be treated as product policy, not an invisible
    optimisation.
\end{description}

interruptions, making this the most evidence-supported next experiment.
It remains a hypothesis until tested through the Kubernetes path.

\section{Limitations}
The simulation matrix is broad but inherits its calibrated/synthetic
scenario assumptions. Bootstrap intervals measure variation across the
designed corpus rather than an external cluster population. The live
study has three sequential repetitions per policy, evolving profiles,
synthetic load-derived thermal signals when sysfs is unavailable, and
worker containers that share one Windows/Docker host. Profile sequences
are non-monotonic and confounded by cyclic position, image/cache state,
and host load. The study validates software mechanisms but not physical
network isolation, real thermal throttling, hardware energy, or node
failure recovery.

The live scheduler receives only ready Pods and currently reconstructs
an empty future-edge list, so no claim about live HEFT upward ranking is
supported. The compared live policies are exact Legacy v1, tuned exact
Legacy, and Adaptive; the Kubernetes default scheduler is not a
baseline in the definitive artifact. Physical \texttt{k3s} scripts and
multi-architecture build paths are implementation assets, not completed
experimental evidence.

\section{Further work}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Repeat the balanced policy experiment on at least three
    independent physical machines, with controlled cross-over order,
    cold and warm profile phases, real link constraints, and hardware
    thermal/energy collection. This is the required external-validation
    step.
    \item Pass the complete Workflow DAG or controller-computed ranks to
    the scheduler, then compare live EFT, HEFT, Legacy, Adaptive, and
    Adaptive without preemption on identical ready frontiers.
    \item Replace threshold-only victim selection with an explicit
    benefit inequality that includes victim progress, restart/startup
    cost, remaining urgent work, and node availability. Add checkpoint
    handoff where the task runtime supports it.
    \item Run live mechanism ablations for profile learning, UCB,
    locality, thermal penalty, failure penalty, and preemption. The
    exact Legacy pair isolates weights, but the Adaptive/Legacy contrast
    changes several mechanisms at once and cannot assign causal effect
    to each score term.
    \item Calibrate simulator uncertainty from repeated physical
    microbenchmarks, then compare predicted task/transfer distributions
    with live observations rather than comparing only policy order.
    Add scheduling latency, isolated slowdown, fairness, and continuous
    thermal exposure only after the harness records them directly.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Closing remarks}
TaskScheduler demonstrates that workflow-aware heterogeneous scheduling
can be carried from explicit models to a functioning Kubernetes control
plane, and that doing so changes the answer. The additional mechanisms
do not produce a universally superior policy. They expose a decision:
whether energy, deadlines, batch completion, or urgent response matters
most, and how much interrupted work the system is permitted to spend on
that objective. Keeping that decision visible is the project's most
defensible conclusion.
