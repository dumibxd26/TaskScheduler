\chapter*{Sinopsis}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Sinopsis}
\begin{otherlanguage}{romanian}
Această lucrare studiază planificarea task-urilor unui workflow (graf
aciclic orientat, DAG) pe un cluster eterogen: ce task gata de execuție
rulează următorul și pe ce nod. Am construit două suprafețe de execuție
care folosesc aceleași descrieri de clustere și workflow-uri: un simulator
cu evenimente discrete, care modelează CPU cu SMT, cache și memorie,
stocare, rețea rutată cu partajare max-min și limite TCP, încălzire și
throttling termic, energie, defecte, checkpoint-uri, gang-uri și
preempțiune, respectiv un plan de control Kubernetes (CRD-uri Workflow și
TaskTemplate, controller de DAG, scheduler propriu, date locale
producătorului, telemetrie de nod). Un audit al versiunii anterioare a
găsit 53 de probleme, dintre care unele schimbau rezultatele; toate au fost
rezolvate, aproape toate prin corecturi de cod acoperite de teste. Pe baza unui eșec măsurat al lui HEFT
(estimări care presupun transferuri fără contenție) propunem HEFT-RA, o
variantă cu estimări conștiente de contenția rețelei, de imaginile
partajate, de încălzire și de defecte, plus o anticipare pe un nivel. Pe
3.750 de cazuri holdout noi, HEFT-RA are regret mediu de 0,37\% și P95 de
0,00\%, față de 12,42\% și 49,02\% pentru HEFT; scorarea implicită
Kubernetes are 160,69\%. Pe clustere Kind care emulează aceleași familii,
o variantă HEFT-RA a fost cea mai rapidă dintre nouă politici pentru
DAG-uri concurente în 8 din 9 rulări, iar politicile care ignoră clasele de
prioritate întârzie task-urile urgente.
Validarea live a scos la iveală și corectat un defect al scheduler-ului, iar
un ``geamăn'' calibrat al simulatorului reproduce timpii live cu o eroare
medie de 3--12\% după modelarea latenței de eliberare a sloturilor.
\end{otherlanguage}

\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

This thesis studies how to schedule the tasks of a workflow, a directed
acyclic graph (DAG) of dependent tasks, on a heterogeneous cluster: which
ready task should run next, and on which node. Two execution surfaces share
one set of cluster and workflow descriptions. The first is a discrete-event
simulator that models CPUs with simultaneous multithreading, caches and
memory bandwidth, storage, routed networks with max-min sharing and TCP
limits, heating and thermal throttling, energy, failures, checkpoints,
gangs, and preemption. The second is a Kubernetes control plane made of
Workflow and TaskTemplate custom resources, a DAG controller, a custom
scheduler, a producer-local data plane, and node telemetry.

An audit of the earlier version of the project found 53 problems, several
of which changed results; all were resolved, almost all by code fixes
covered by regression tests.
A measured failure of HEFT, whose estimates assume that transfers never
compete for a link, motivated HEFT-RA: HEFT's task order with
finish-time estimates that account for network contention, shared image
pulls, heating, and expected failure loss, plus a one-level lookahead. On
3,750 fresh holdout cases HEFT-RA has 0.37\% mean and 0.00\% P95
makespan regret, against 12.42\% and 49.02\% for HEFT and 160.69\% mean
regret for a model of the default Kubernetes scoring. With a
capacity-aware lookahead it also leads the overlapping-workload
experiments on median and tail response regret and deadline hit rate,
while a preemptive priority policy remains the specialist for urgent
bursts. On same-host Kind clusters that emulate the same cluster families,
a HEFT-RA variant was the fastest of nine policies for concurrent DAGs in
8 of 9 runs (makespan 0.917 of HEFT's), and policies that ignore priority
classes delay urgent work. The live validation also exposed and fixed a scheduler defect
that froze nodes for up to 30 seconds, and a calibrated simulator twin
reproduces live times within 3--12\% mean absolute error (3--8\% on the
heterogeneous clusters) once the measured slot-release latency is modelled.

\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:intro}

\section{Context}

Scheduling in a distributed system is the repeated decision of \emph{when}
and \emph{where} a unit of work runs, under resource limits and under
objectives such as latency, throughput, utilisation, and fairness. Two
properties of modern clusters make this decision harder than it looks.

First, clusters are heterogeneous. A cluster may combine nodes optimised
for CPU throughput, nodes with wide memory buses, nodes with fast local
storage, and small edge devices connected through Wi-Fi or a wide-area
link. The same task can take a few seconds on one node and several times
longer on another, and moving its input data can cost more than running it.

Second, many applications are not single jobs but \emph{workflows}:
several stages connected by data dependencies, naturally modelled as a
directed acyclic graph (DAG). In Kubernetes~\cite{kubernetes}, workflow
engines such as Argo Workflows~\cite{argo-workflows,argo-dag} decide which
task becomes eligible once its parents finish, but the decision of where
each Pod runs is made by kube-scheduler~\cite{kubernetes-scheduler,
kubernetes-framework}, one Pod at a time, without knowledge of the DAG or
of the workflow's end-to-end goal.

\section{Problem}

We consider an online stream of workflow executions on a heterogeneous
Kubernetes cluster. The number of workflow \emph{types} is limited, but
each type recurs many times, and several instances can run concurrently.
The problem therefore has two levels:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{per workflow}: minimise the end-to-end time (makespan) of each
      instance, using DAG context such as the critical path and avoiding
      unnecessary data movement and start-up delays;
\item \textbf{per system}: when many instances run at the same time,
      improve mean and tail response time and throughput, under contention
      and under priorities between workflows.
\end{itemize}

The central question is: \emph{how should the tasks of a workflow be
ordered and placed on heterogeneous nodes so that end-to-end latency is
minimised, respecting compatibility constraints and contention?}

Following the original project description we separate two notions that
share the word ``locality''. \emph{Data locality} is about where a task's
inputs and outputs are, and what it costs to move them along the DAG's
edges~\cite{zaharia2010delay,isard2009quincy}. \emph{Image locality} is
about whether a container image is already present on a node, which
affects start-up latency~\cite{kubernetes-config}. The project models
both, which turned out to be essential (Chapter~\ref{ch:eval}).

\section{Motivation: why Argo and kube-scheduler are not enough}

Argo executes a DAG according to its dependencies but does not optimise
placement. kube-scheduler places Pods using resource requests and
declarative rules (affinity, taints, tolerations) and scores nodes with
plugins such as \texttt{NodeResourcesFit}, \texttt{BalancedAllocation} and
\texttt{ImageLocality}~\cite{kubernetes-config}. It has no access to:

\begin{itemize}
\item the role of a task in the DAG (its criticality, its position on the
      critical path);
\item the end-to-end objective (the workflow's makespan rather than a good
      local placement);
\item processor speed differences between nodes, the location and size of
      the task's inputs, and the current state of links and nodes.
\end{itemize}

Local decisions can therefore lengthen the critical path, move data that
need not move, and introduce avoidable cold starts. Our experiments
quantify this: a faithful model of the default scoring has a mean makespan
regret of 162.56\% on our holdout cases (Section~\ref{sec:closed-results}).

\section{Objectives}

\begin{enumerate}
\item Define an executable model for the online execution of many DAG
      workflow instances on a heterogeneous cluster, detailed enough to
      reproduce the effects that decide placement quality (processor speed,
      memory and cache behaviour, storage, network contention, heating,
      failures, start-up cost).
\item Design a workflow-to-scheduler interface that carries DAG structure,
      priority, gang membership, and data provenance to the scheduler.
\item Implement and compare workflow-aware placement policies, from simple
      controls to HEFT-style list scheduling, against a model of the
      default Kubernetes behaviour.
\item Implement an online learning loop (profiles of runtime and start-up
      per task template and node, with exploration) for recurring
      workflows.
\item Study system-level behaviour under concurrency: priorities,
      preemption, and their cost to other workflows.
\item Validate the simulator's conclusions on a real Kubernetes control
      plane running emulated heterogeneous clusters, and recommend a
      policy per workflow type and per cluster type.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research questions and hypotheses}
\label{sec:rqs}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2cm,style=nextline]
\item[RQ1] Can a workflow-aware scheduler that uses DAG signals reduce
      makespan compared with local, Pod-by-Pod placement?\\
      \emph{H1: list scheduling with upward ranks (HEFT family) has lower
      makespan regret than the default Kubernetes scoring.}
\item[RQ2] With many concurrent workflow instances, does the approach also
      improve system-level performance (mean and tail response,
      deadlines)?\\
      \emph{H2: the policy that is best for single workflows is also best,
      or close to best, for overlapping workloads.}
\item[RQ3] How much does cold-start-aware placement (image locality,
      shared pulls, pre-warming) contribute to time-to-running and
      makespan?\\
      \emph{H3: image-aware estimates reduce time-to-running; the
      remaining cold-start cost is bounded by a pre-warmed cluster.}
\item[RQ4] Does online learning (explore/exploit) make recurring workflows
      cheaper to schedule well?\\
      \emph{H4: warm profiles lead to better placements than cold
      profiles, and tuning the scorer helps most when profiles are cold.}
\item[RQ5] Do priorities and preemption reduce the degradation of critical
      workflows without severely harming the others?\\
      \emph{H5: preemption shortens urgent response at a small cost to
      non-urgent work.}
\end{description}

A sixth, methodological question runs through the whole thesis:
\textbf{RQ6} --- \emph{do conclusions drawn in simulation survive on a
real control plane?}

\section{Proposed solution}

The solution adds a workflow-aware layer to Kubernetes and pairs it with
a simulator that can evaluate thousands of scheduling situations:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Workflow and TaskTemplate custom resources} carry the DAG,
      priorities, gang sizes, and data contracts; a controller creates
      Pods only for ready tasks and annotates them with DAG context.
\item \textbf{A custom scheduler} orders ready Pods and scores nodes with
      DAG ranks, learned runtime profiles, measured bandwidths, data
      provenance, and image presence; it binds Pods through the API and
      can preempt lower-priority work.
\item \textbf{A producer-local data plane}: outputs stay on the node that
      produced them and are fetched by consumers, so data locality has a
      real cost.
\item \textbf{HEFT-RA}, a resource-aware variant of HEFT, designed after
      the simulator exposed why HEFT fails on contention-heavy cases.
\item \textbf{A same-host emulation} of heterogeneous clusters on Kind,
      and a \textbf{calibrated simulator twin} of each emulated cluster,
      to test whether simulated conclusions hold on the live system.
\end{itemize}

\section{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item A validated, deterministic discrete-event simulator for DAG
      scheduling on heterogeneous clusters, with directional and
      half-duplex links, TCP loss and window limits, SMT-aware CPU
      capacity, cache-aware memory traffic, an RC thermal model, energy,
      independent failure streams, gang-atomic recovery, and a
      control-plane delay.
\item HEFT-RA, a weight-free extension of HEFT whose estimates account
      for network contention (in-flight and same-round flows), shared image
      pulls, thermal throttling, and expected failure loss, with a
      one-level lookahead that, in its final form, only counts on capacity
      the children will actually find.
\item A multi-phase evaluation (exploratory, confirmatory, holdout, and a
      fresh holdout for the final policy set) with per-seed perturbation of
      every hardware and network parameter, which fixes the
      non-independence of seeds in the earlier evaluation.
\item A corrected Kubernetes control plane (53 audited findings, resolved
      with code fixes and regression tests or, for packaging issues, with
      corrected documentation), live implementations of all compared
      policies, and an emulation of heterogeneous clusters on one PC with
      calibrated speeds, CPU-time work budgets, and shaped links.
\item Recommendations per workflow family and per cluster family, backed
      by holdout evidence and by live validation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{A note on the history of this work}

An earlier version of this project reached different conclusions (HEFT as
the best closed-DAG policy, Adaptive as the response-time specialist, and
a Kubernetes comparison that favoured Adaptive). A systematic audit found
that some of those conclusions rested on defects: a lower bound that was
not a bound, random failures that depended on the scheduling policy,
seeds that repeated the same experiment, and a live emulation in which
every node ran tasks at the same speed. This thesis reports the corrected
system and results; Appendix~\ref{app:corrections} lists what changed and
why, and the engineering journal in the repository tells the story in
detail.

\section{Structure of the thesis}

Chapter~\ref{ch:sota} reviews DAG scheduling, cluster schedulers,
gang scheduling, admission control, locality, cold starts, and the
performance models the simulator builds on. Chapter~\ref{ch:arch}
describes the architecture: the model, the simulator, the policies
(including HEFT-RA), the Kubernetes control plane, the emulation, and the
twin. Chapter~\ref{ch:eval} presents the experimental protocol and
results, both simulated and live, and answers the research questions.
Chapter~\ref{ch:limits} discusses limitations and future work, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} concludes.

\chapter{State of the Art}
\label{ch:sota}

\section{Workflow (DAG) scheduling on heterogeneous resources}

Scheduling a DAG on a set of processors to minimise makespan is
NP-hard even in simple settings~\cite{ullman1975np}, so practical
schedulers are heuristics. The most studied family is \emph{list
scheduling}: tasks are given priorities, then repeatedly the
highest-priority ready task is assigned to the processor that optimises a
cost function. Kwok and Ahmad~\cite{kwok1999static} survey the classic
static algorithms.

HEFT (Heterogeneous Earliest Finish Time)~\cite{topcuoglu2002heft} is the
reference algorithm for heterogeneous processors. Each task's
\emph{upward rank} is its average execution time plus the maximum, over its
children, of the average communication time to the child plus the child's
rank; the rank therefore approximates the length of the remaining
critical path. Tasks are processed in decreasing rank order and each is
placed on the processor with the earliest estimated finish time (EFT),
including the time to receive its inputs. HEFT is simple, fast, and hard
to beat consistently, which is why it is the standard baseline.

HEFT's weaknesses are well documented. Its placement is greedy, so a
task may be put where it finishes early but its children cannot. PEFT
uses an optimistic cost table to look ahead~\cite{arabnejad2014peft},
and Bittencourt et al.~\cite{bittencourt2010lookahead} evaluate each
placement by the finish times of the task's children, a one-level
lookahead that we adopt. HEFT also assumes a \emph{contention-free}
communication model: every transfer is estimated as if it had the link to
itself. Sinnen and Sousa~\cite{sinnen2005contention} showed that ignoring
contention can make schedules far longer than predicted and proposed
contention-aware list scheduling over an explicit network graph. This
thesis meets the same problem in a measured form
(Section~\ref{sec:heft-failure}).

For workflows in clouds and clusters, Rodriguez and
Buyya~\cite{rodriguez2017taxonomy} classify algorithms by objective
(makespan, cost, deadline), by when decisions are taken (static,
dynamic, hybrid), and by the resource model. Characterisation studies of
real scientific workflows~\cite{bharathi2008characterization,
juve2013characterizing} identify recurring structures (pipelines,
fan-out/data distribution, fan-in/aggregation, diamonds) that our
workflow corpus reproduces.

\section{The workflow-to-scheduler interface}

A scheduler can only be workflow-aware if someone tells it about the
workflow. Lehmann et al.~\cite{lehmann2023cwsi,lehmann2023status}
propose the Common Workflow Scheduler Interface (CWSI), a contract through
which workflow engines expose DAG structure and task metadata to resource
managers, so that scheduling can be separated from orchestration. In
Kubernetes, the natural vehicles for such metadata are custom resources
(CRDs)~\cite{kubernetes-crd} and annotations, consumed either by a plugin
of the scheduling framework~\cite{kubernetes-framework} or by a
secondary scheduler selected through \texttt{schedulerName}. This thesis
uses CRDs for workflows and templates, and Pod annotations for the
information the scheduler needs (DAG, ranks, gang size, priority, data
provenance).

\section{Gang co-scheduling for fork-join stages}

When the tasks of a stage synchronise, starting only some of them wastes
resources and lets stragglers lengthen the critical path. Gang
scheduling~\cite{feitelson1992gang} admits a group of tasks together or
not at all. Kubernetes has long relied on external projects for this
(Volcano~\cite{volcano}, the co-scheduling plugin); recent releases add a
Workload API and gang scheduling support~\cite{k8s-workload-api,
k8s-gang,k8s-wa-blog}. Our simulator and live scheduler treat a gang as a
single scheduling unit: it is placed only if all members fit, and in the
simulator a failure of any member restarts the whole gang.

\section{Admission control and queueing}

In a shared cluster, \emph{which} workflow is admitted can matter as much
as where its next Pod runs. Kueue~\cite{kueue-blog,kueue-docs} separates
admission (quotas, fair sharing, queueing) from Pod placement. Classic
cluster managers combine admission and placement with fairness policies:
Borg~\cite{verma2015borg} uses priorities and quotas, Omega~\cite{
schwarzkopf2013omega} lets several schedulers share state optimistically,
Sparrow~\cite{ousterhout2013sparrow} schedules short tasks with sampling,
and Dominant Resource Fairness~\cite{ghodsi2011drf} generalises max-min
fairness to multiple resources. Our live engine admits workflows into a
queue ordered by priority and arrival and uses Kubernetes-style priority
classes; quota-based admission is left for future work.

\section{Data locality versus image locality}

Data-intensive schedulers try to run tasks close to their data. Delay
scheduling~\cite{zaharia2010delay} waits briefly for a slot on a node that
holds the input; Quincy~\cite{isard2009quincy} formulates locality and
fairness as a min-cost flow. For DAGs, the cost of moving data along an
edge is part of the finish-time estimate itself, as in HEFT. Image
locality is a different effect: whether a container image is cached on the
node. kube-scheduler's \texttt{ImageLocality} plugin favours nodes that
already hold the image, scaled by image size and by how widely the image
is spread~\cite{kubernetes-config}. Packing schedulers such as
Tetris~\cite{grandl2014tetris} show that multi-resource packing, rather
than per-task placement, can also reduce job completion times; this is
relevant to the one case where our list schedulers lose to simple packing
(Section~\ref{sec:closed-results}).

\section{Cold starts and image distribution}

For short tasks and latency-sensitive pipelines, start-up overhead (image
pull, container creation, cold caches) can dominate. Lazy pulling, for
example the stargz snapshotter~\cite{stargz}, starts a container before its
image is fully downloaded, and peer-to-peer distribution such as
Dragonfly~\cite{dragonfly} spreads the load of image downloads. Our model
represents images explicitly (size, registry link, per-node cache) and lets
several tasks on a node share one pull.

\section{Modelling heterogeneous hardware for simulation}

Running thousands of scheduling experiments requires a simulator, and a
simulator is only useful if it captures the effects that decide placement
quality. The models used here come from well-established work:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Processors.} Execution time follows calibrated per-core
      throughput, Amdahl's law for the parallel fraction~\cite{amdahl1967},
      and a roofline-style bound when memory traffic dominates
      compute~\cite{williams2009roofline}. Simultaneous multithreading adds
      only a fraction of a core's throughput~\cite{tullsen1995smt}; caches
      absorb part of the memory traffic depending on the working set and
      its reuse~\cite{hennessy2017ca}.
\item \textbf{Networks.} Flow-level simulators such as
      SimGrid~\cite{casanova2014simgrid} share link capacity between active
      flows by max-min fairness~\cite{bertsekas1992data}; Velho et
      al.~\cite{velho2013flow} showed that such models are accurate for
      bulk transfers once contention and round-trip effects are modelled.
      A single TCP flow is further limited by its window divided by the
      round-trip time and, under random loss, by the bound of Mathis et
      al.~\cite{mathis1997tcp}; modern congestion control such as
      CUBIC~\cite{ha2008cubic} does somewhat better on long, lossy paths.
\item \textbf{Heat and power.} A lumped thermal resistance/capacitance
      model, as in HotSpot~\cite{skadron2003temperature}, gives an
      exponential approach to a steady-state temperature that depends on
      power; above a threshold the processor throttles. Server power is
      well approximated as idle power plus a term linear in
      utilisation~\cite{fan2007power}. Temperature-aware placement has been
      studied at data-centre scale~\cite{moore2005cool}.
\item \textbf{Failures.} Field studies show that failure rates differ
      strongly between nodes and that failures are not always
      independent~\cite{schroeder2010failures}; we use per-node Poisson
      failures plus scripted fault injection. Checkpointing bounds the work
      lost to a failure~\cite{young1974checkpoint}.
\end{itemize}

For the live emulation we rely on Linux control groups (CPU quotas and
weights)~\cite{cgroup-v2} and on \texttt{tc netem}~\cite{hemminger2005netem}
for link delay and rate, inside Kind~\cite{kind-quickstart}, which runs
Kubernetes nodes as containers.

\section{Learning and adaptive scheduling}

When execution times are not known in advance, schedulers learn them.
Bandit algorithms such as UCB~\cite{auer2002ucb} balance exploring
uncertain options and exploiting good ones; the project's earlier Adaptive
policy uses a UCB-style optimism term over learned profiles. Deep
reinforcement learning has been used to learn DAG scheduling policies for
data-processing clusters (Decima~\cite{mao2019decima}). Such policies need
large amounts of training data and are hard to inspect; for a thesis whose
aim is to explain \emph{why} a policy wins, we prefer analytical estimators
with explicit models and use learning only for the quantities that must be
measured (runtimes, start-up times, bandwidths).

\chapter{Architecture and Implementation}
\label{ch:arch}

\section{Overview}

The system has two execution surfaces that read the same kind of
description and implement the same policies (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}). The
\emph{simulator} answers comparative questions cheaply: thousands of
(cluster, workflow, policy, seed) runs per minute, deterministically. The
\emph{Kubernetes control plane} answers whether the ideas work on a real
API server, kubelets, cgroups, and network stack. A \emph{twin} connects

\section{Model: workflow, task, and signals}

All scenarios are YAML files validated by \texttt{simulation/schema.py}.

\paragraph{Nodes.} A node has a CPU (architecture, physical cores, threads
per core, base and maximum frequency, calibrated GFLOP/s per core, L3 size,
idle and maximum power, SMT efficiency), memory (capacity, calibrated
bandwidth, latency, channels, optional per-task bandwidth limit), storage
(sequential and random read/write rates, optional per-task limits), a
thermal description (ambient, initial, throttle and shutdown temperatures,
thermal resistance and capacitance, minimum throttle factor), optional
reliability parameters (Poisson failure rate, scripted failure times, mean
repair time), and background load. Table~\ref{tab:nodes} shows an example.

\paragraph{Links.} A link connects two vertices (nodes, switches, or the
image registry) and has bandwidth, latency, jitter, a TCP loss-event rate,
a medium (\texttt{ethernet}, \texttt{wifi}, \texttt{wan}), an optional
reverse bandwidth and latency for asymmetric links, and a duplex mode
(Wi-Fi defaults to half duplex). Routes are shortest paths over this graph.

\paragraph{Tasks.} A task declares its compute work (GFLOP) and parallel
fraction, memory reads and writes with a working-set size and cache-reuse
fraction, disk reads and writes with a random-I/O fraction and block size,
CPU cores and memory requested, its container image and image size,
start-up time, supported architectures, priority class, deadline,
preemptability, checkpoint interval, release time, and an optional gang
group. Dependencies carry the number of MiB that flow along the edge.

\paragraph{Workloads.} A workload is a list of workflow submissions with
arrival times, priorities, preemptability, and deadlines; it is compiled
into a single DAG whose task identifiers are prefixed by the submission.

\section{The discrete-event simulator}
\label{sec:simulator}

\subsection{Event loop}

The engine (\texttt{simulation/engine.py}) keeps virtual time and jumps
from event to event: task releases, the end of an image pull or input
transfer, the end of an execution, a node reaching its throttle
temperature, failures and repairs, and checkpoints. Between events, every
active execution and transfer progresses at a constant rate; whenever the
set of activities changes, all rates are recomputed. At each scheduling
point the policy receives a read-only snapshot (ready tasks, node states,
temperatures, warm images, running tasks, transfers in flight) and returns
placements. A placed task first pulls its image if the node does not have
it, then receives its inputs, then executes.

\subsection{Processors, memory, and storage}

A task's execution time on a node is the maximum of four independent
ceilings, in the spirit of the roofline model~\cite{williams2009roofline}:
\begin{equation}
t_{\mathrm{exec}} = \max\left(
\frac{W}{r_{\mathrm{cpu}}},\;
\frac{M \cdot m(S, \rho, L3)}{B_{\mathrm{dram}}},\;
\frac{D_r}{B_{\mathrm{read}}},\;
\frac{D_w}{B_{\mathrm{write}}}\right),
\end{equation}
where $W$ is compute work, $r_{\mathrm{cpu}}$ the task's CPU rate, $M$ its
memory traffic, $m$ the fraction of that traffic that misses the L3 cache
(a function of working set $S$, reuse $\rho$ and cache size), and $D_r$,
$D_w$ its disk traffic. The CPU rate follows Amdahl's law for the
requested cores and the task's parallel fraction, is scaled by the thermal
throttle factor, and is limited by the node-wide capacity, which includes
the SMT gain: a node with $p$ physical cores of $g$ GFLOP/s and SMT
efficiency $e$ offers at most $p\,g\,(1+e)$ when all hardware threads are
busy. Concurrent tasks share CPU, DRAM bandwidth and storage by
progressive filling, with optional per-task limits. Random I/O is limited
by IOPS times block size.

\subsection{Network}

Each transfer is a fluid TCP flow along its route. A hop maps to a
\emph{channel}: one per direction on full-duplex media, one shared channel
on half-duplex media. Rates are allocated by progressive max-min
filling~\cite{bertsekas1992data} over channels, and each flow is
additionally limited by its receive window divided by the round-trip time
and by the Mathis bound~\cite{mathis1997tcp}
\begin{equation}
r \le \frac{\mathit{MSS}\,\sqrt{3/2}}{\mathit{RTT}\,\sqrt{p}},
\end{equation}
where $p$ is the combined loss-event rate along the route. A protocol
efficiency of 0.97 accounts for header overhead, and per-transfer jitter
is drawn from the transfer's own random stream. Image pulls are flows
from the registry vertex.

\subsection{Heat, power, energy}

Each node follows a first-order RC model~\cite{skadron2003temperature}:
with power $P$, ambient $T_a$, resistance $R$ and capacitance $C$, the
temperature approaches $T_a + P R$ exponentially with time constant
$RC$. Power grows from idle to maximum with the share of busy hardware
threads along a mildly convex curve (exponent 1.4), a refinement of the
near-linear relation reported for servers~\cite{fan2007power}. Above the
throttle temperature the CPU rate is
reduced linearly towards the minimum throttle factor at the shutdown
temperature. The engine computes the exact time at which a node will cross
its throttle point and schedules an event there, so throttling starts at
the right moment rather than at the next periodic step. Energy is the
integral of power over time.

\subsection{Failures, checkpoints, gangs, priorities}

Nodes fail according to a Poisson process at their declared rate and/or
at scripted times, and are repaired after a sampled delay. Each node draws
its failures from its own random stream, derived from the run seed and the
node identifier, so that two policies run on the same seed experience
exactly the same failures. A failure interrupts the node's tasks; a
checkpointable task resumes from its last checkpoint, others restart. A
gang is scheduled only when all its members can start, and a failure of
any member restarts the whole gang. Priority classes (from
\texttt{BEST\_EFFORT} through \texttt{BATCH} and \texttt{REAL\_TIME\_MEDIUM}
to \texttt{CRITICAL}) order tasks
for priority-aware policies, and preemptive policies may interrupt
preemptable lower-priority tasks. A cluster-level control-plane delay
models the time between a task becoming ready and becoming placeable.

\subsection{Uncertainty and seeds}

A seed controls (i) a perturbation of the cluster's hardware and network
parameters, (ii) jitter, and (iii) random failures. Each cluster declares a
calibration uncertainty $u$; the experiment runner can impose a floor on
it. Every node's per-core throughput, DRAM bandwidth and latency, storage
rates and IOPS, idle and dynamic power, thermal resistance and
capacitance, failure rate and repair time, and every link's bandwidth,
latency and jitter are then multiplied by independent seeded factors drawn
uniformly from $[1-u, 1+u]$; structural parameters (core counts, cache
sizes, temperature thresholds, loss rates) are unchanged. With a floor of
0.1, every seed is therefore a distinct instance of the declared cluster.
Section~\ref{sec:protocol} explains why this floor was necessary.

\subsection{Lower bound}

For sanity checks the runner computes a lower bound on any achievable
makespan: the maximum of the critical path (each task on its fastest
compatible node, unthrottled, with a private L3) and four aggregate terms
(total compute over total SMT-aware CPU capacity, total DRAM traffic after
cache hits over total DRAM bandwidth, total reads and writes over total
storage rates). Every term is optimistic, so the bound is valid.

\section{The workflow-to-scheduler interface}

Two custom resources define the live API (\texttt{crds/}).
A \textbf{TaskTemplate} describes a reusable task: image and pull policy,
task class (CPU-, memory-, I/O-bound), logical CPU and memory request,
optional physical resources and emulation scales, compatible node types,
command, arguments, environment, expected output bytes, checkpointability,
and gang group. A \textbf{Workflow} lists tasks (each referring to a
template) with typed dependencies, plus a priority, a workflow class, and
preemptability.

The controller creates a Pod only when all of a task's parents have
succeeded, sets \texttt{schedulerName} to the custom scheduler, and passes
the context the scheduler needs as annotations: the compact DAG of the
whole workflow (tasks, templates, typed edges with expected bytes), the
task's class, compatible node types, logical requests, priority,
preemptability, gang group and gang size, and the producer node and size
of every input. Tasks receive environment variables for their identity,
their input and output directories, their node name (downward API), and
the emulated speed table (an optional ConfigMap).

\section{Workflow-aware policies}
\label{sec:policies}

\subsection{Controls and the default-Kubernetes model}

FCFS places each ready task on the first node that fits; Random picks a
seeded random feasible node. \pol{k8s-default} models the default
kube-scheduler profile: Pods are taken in PrioritySort order (priority,
then readiness time) and placed on the feasible node with the highest sum
of NodeResourcesFit (LeastAllocated), NodeResourcesBalancedAllocation, and
ImageLocality scores, with the default thresholds of 23 MiB and 1000 MiB
for image size and the spread scaling, and random tie-breaking. It sees
requests and image presence only; it is blind to processor speed, data
location, temperature and failures.

\subsection{EFT and HEFT}

EFT takes ready tasks in readiness order and places each on the node with
the earliest estimated finish time. HEFT~\cite{topcuoglu2002heft} orders
ready tasks by upward rank
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{rank}_u(t) = \overline{w}(t) + \max_{c \in \mathrm{succ}(t)}
\left(\overline{c}(t, c) + \mathrm{rank}_u(c)\right),
\end{equation}
where $\overline{w}$ is the task's average execution time over nodes and
$\overline{c}$ the average communication time of the edge. Both use the
same estimator: start-up (including an image pull if needed), the slowest
input transfer at the route's uncontended rate, and the execution time on
that node next to the tasks already running there.

\subsection{HEFT-RA}
\label{sec:heftra}

HEFT-RA keeps HEFT's order and replaces its estimate. It was designed after
the simulator showed that HEFT's contention-free estimate fails badly on
data-heavy fan-outs (Section~\ref{sec:heft-failure}). Its estimate of the
finish time of task $t$ on node $n$ differs in four ways:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Contended transfers.} Every input transfer receives a max-min
      share of each channel on its route, given the transfers already in
      flight \emph{and} those planned earlier in the same scheduling round,
      and is capped by the TCP window and loss bounds.
\item \textbf{Shared image pulls.} If the image is already being pulled to
      $n$, or a pull was planned there in this round, the task does not pay
      for a second pull.
\item \textbf{Thermal prediction.} CPU work is integrated over the RC model
      for the predicted duration, so a node that will heat past its
      throttle point during a long task is charged for the slowdown.
\item \textbf{Expected failure loss.} On a node with failure rate
      $\lambda$, a task of duration $d$ fails with probability
      $1-e^{-\lambda d}$; the expected delay (half the exposed work, at
      most half a checkpoint interval for checkpointable tasks, plus the
      restart cost) is added.
\end{enumerate}

Finally, a producer is scored with a one-level
lookahead~\cite{bittencourt2010lookahead}: for every child, the best
(contended transfer + execution) over all nodes is computed with the
siblings pulling from the producer concurrently, and the producer's score
is the latest child finish. Listing~\ref{lst:heftra} summarises one
scheduling round. HEFT-RA has no weights or tuned parameters; any
difference from HEFT is due to the estimator.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={One HEFT-RA scheduling round.},label={lst:heftra},language={}]
flows   <- transfers in flight;  pulls <- image pulls in flight
for unit in scheduling_units(ready tasks sorted by upward rank):
    for each task t in unit, for each feasible node n:
        own  <- start-up(t, n, pulls)
              + contended_transfer(inputs(t) -> n, flows)
              + thermal_execution(t, n, co-runners)
              + expected_failure_delay(t, n)
        look <- max over children c of t:
                  own + min over nodes m of
                      contended_transfer(n -> m, siblings) + exec(c, m)
        score(t, n) <- max(own, look)
    place the unit on the best feasible assignment (all-or-nothing)
    flows <- flows + planned input flows;  pulls <- pulls + planned pulls
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{HEFT-RA with a capacity-aware lookahead}
\label{sec:heftra-cap}

The first live trials showed a weakness of the lookahead that the wide
simulated nodes had hidden: it assumes that every child can run on its
best node, even when that node offers only one or two task slots and other
work is about to take them. \pol{heft-ra-cap} keeps everything else in
HEFT-RA and changes only the lookahead. The children of a producer are
assigned the way the non-delay list scheduler will actually place them:
each goes to the best node that still has a free slot in the capacity left
after the current round's commitments; a child waits for an earlier
sibling only when every node is full; and only when no node has any
capacity does the estimate fall back to the optimistic bound. Because the
lookahead now needs the children's resource requests, which the live
scheduler sees only once their Pods exist, a child's template is looked up
in any workflow that used it, and otherwise is assumed to be as large as
its producer. On the simulated clusters, whose nodes run 8 to 16 tasks at
a time, both variants make identical decisions for single workflows
(Section~\ref{sec:closed-results}).

\subsection{The Legacy scorer}

The project's original live scheduler scores each feasible node with a
weighted sum of eight normalised factors: resource fit, node availability,
load balance, data locality, type affinity, warm image, failure penalty,
and memory pressure. \pol{legacy-v1} uses the original weights;
\pol{legacy-tuned} uses weights found by a seeded random search (256
log-normal candidates at a fixed absolute scale of 125, with training,
validation and holdout splits and guardrails on retries, deadlines and
P95 makespan). Both enforce three observations per compatible node before
exploiting, and explore at random 10\% of the time afterwards.
\pol{legacy} is a historical simulator approximation kept for continuity.

\subsection{Adaptive}

\pol{adaptive} orders tasks by priority class and then by upward rank, and
scores nodes by the EFT estimate plus penalties for energy (0.002 s/J),
transfer volume (0.001 s/MiB), thermal pressure (up to 10 s), and failure
probability (100 s times the probability). It may preempt lower-priority
preemptable work for urgent tasks. \pol{adaptive-no-preempt} is identical
with preemption disabled. Live, Adaptive also adds a UCB-style optimism
term over learned runtimes~\cite{auer2002ucb}.

\section{Cold-start-aware scheduling}

Images are first-class in both surfaces. In the simulator a cold start is
a flow of the image's size from the registry over the routed network,
shared fairly with data flows; a node caches every image it has pulled.
EFT, HEFT and Adaptive include the pull in their estimate; HEFT-RA also
avoids paying twice for a pull already in progress;
\pol{k8s-default} uses ImageLocality. The live scheduler learns start-up
time per template and node in its profiles, which includes the pull when
the image is absent. Section~\ref{sec:mechanisms} measures how much of
the makespan is due to cold starts and how much image awareness recovers.

\section{Stage-level gang co-scheduling}

Both surfaces group ready tasks of the same gang into one scheduling unit.
A unit is placed only if every member fits (the simulator searches member
assignments with branch and bound; scores are shifted to be non-negative
so that pruning on partial sums is valid), otherwise all members wait.
Placement reservations of a unit are rolled back if any member cannot be
placed. In the simulator, a node failure that interrupts one member
interrupts the whole gang.

\section{Learning loop: profiles, coverage, exploration}

The live scheduler keeps a profile per (task template, node): a rolling
window of runtime and start-up observations whose median is used as the
estimate, an exponential moving average ($\alpha = 0.3$) used to detect
drift, a count, and failures. Profiles are persisted and restored without
replaying observations over the stored average. \emph{Coverage}: until a
template has three observations on every compatible node, the least
sampled node is chosen; afterwards policies exploit. Legacy keeps 10\%
random exploration; Adaptive adds UCB optimism ($\beta = 1$). The
simulator gives the Legacy variants seeded profiles (12 observations per
task and node, with the cluster's uncertainty) and compares cold and warm
profiles in the weight search.

\section{Admission, priorities, and preemption}

The live engine admits workflows into a queue ordered by priority class
and arrival, and orders ready tasks by effective priority and enqueue
time; a \texttt{REAL\_TIME\_MEDIUM} task that waits too long is promoted
(aging). Each tick (1 s) the policy receives the ready Pods, the
cluster state, and the profiles, and returns placements, holds, and
preemptions. No policy overcommits a node; a CRITICAL task that does not
fit waits until resources are released. Preemption is explicit
kill-and-restart, as in Kubernetes' own Pod
preemption~\cite{kubernetes-preemption}: a victim must be strictly lower
priority and
preemptable, a per-node budget limits how many victims (including ones
already planned in the same tick) a node can lose in a window, and a
victim is never chosen twice. A preempted task enters a dedicated
\texttt{PREEMPTED} state and is re-queued only when the controller has
created its replacement Pod, which prevents the double dispatch found in
the earlier version.

\section{The live control plane}

\paragraph{Controller.} Watches Workflows and Pods, creates Pods for ready
tasks with collision-free names (a readable prefix plus a hash of
workflow, task and UID), propagates failures to descendants, retries
failed tasks, and records per-task timestamps (ready, Pod created, started,
finished, attempts) and the admission and finish time of each workflow.
Task output is parsed strictly; a malformed output marks the task failed
instead of wedging it.

\paragraph{Scheduler.} Watches pending Pods that name it, builds task
entries from annotations (including upward ranks computed from the
published DAG), runs the policy each tick, and binds through the API. A
bind has three outcomes (success, retry, drop) and the reservation is
rolled back on anything but success. Cluster state is refreshed from the
API outside the scheduling lock and merged with the scheduler's own
reservations, so a refresh cannot erase decisions that the API has not
yet reflected. The merge also skips Pods whose completion or preemption
the scheduler has already processed: the inventory is polled outside the
lock, so a Pod that finished between the poll and the merge still looks
running in it, and charging it again froze its node until the next
refresh 30~seconds later (Section~\ref{sec:live}). One lock protects all
shared state.

\paragraph{Data plane and telemetry.} Outputs are written under the
producing node's \texttt{/var/lib/ts-data} and served by a per-node
fileserver; a task fetches its parents' outputs at start and reports how
long that took. A bandwidth probe DaemonSet measures transfers from every
peer in staggered time slots and annotates nodes with the measured rates;
a thermal DaemonSet reports real sensor temperatures where they exist.

\section{Same-host emulation of heterogeneous clusters}
\label{sec:emulation}

The validation clusters run on one laptop (Intel Core i5-8300H, 4 cores /
8 threads, 12 GB RAM~\cite{intel-i5-8300h}) under Docker Desktop. Virtual
machines were rejected: they share the same four cores, so a ``fast'' VM
cannot be faster than the host, and each would cost gigabytes of memory.
Instead, \texttt{kind\_lab.py} builds a Kind cluster from a profile in
\texttt{clusters/kind\_profiles.yaml} and emulates heterogeneity in four
ways:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Slots.} Every validation task requests and is limited to
      500m CPU, so a worker with $k$ slots runs $k$ tasks at a time.
\item \textbf{Container caps.} Each worker container gets
      $0.5k + 0.35$ CPUs (the extra 0.35 for node agents), $224k + 576$
      MiB of memory, and CPU shares proportional to $k$.
\item \textbf{Speeds.} A ConfigMap gives each node a CPU, memory and I/O
      speed. CPU and memory tasks receive a \emph{CPU-time budget} and keep
      working until they have consumed budget$/$speed CPU-seconds; I/O
      tasks write real bytes at a paced rate scaled by the I/O speed.
\item \textbf{Links.} \texttt{tc netem}~\cite{hemminger2005netem} on each
      worker's egress sets delay and rate; since outputs stay on their
      producer, the producer's egress shapes every transfer it serves.
\end{enumerate}

CPU-time budgets replaced an earlier design that counted iterations.
Calibration showed that the same number of iterations ran at 0.99--1.09
million hashes per second on single-slot nodes but only 0.63 million on a
two-slot node, because concurrent tasks shared physical cores through SMT
and containers shared the host by cgroup weight~\cite{cgroup-v2}: the
``fast'' node was slower per task than the ``slow'' one. Consumed CPU time
is insensitive to this effect, so a task's duration depends only on its
budget, its node's speed, and its quota. After the change, a CPU task
on a node with CPU speed 0.45 reported 6.83~s for a 3.33~CPU-second
budget, i.e.\ 3.07~s of speed-normalised work against 3.0~s expected.

\section{The calibrated simulator twin}
\label{sec:twin}

\texttt{simulation/kind\_twin.py} turns an emulated cluster into a
simulator cluster: each worker becomes a node whose cores equal its
slots and whose per-class rates follow the speed table; each link becomes
a directional link with the netem delay and the \emph{measured} egress
rate. The twin is calibrated only on the warm-up phase of each live run:
the base work of each template is the median of (runtime $-$ staging time)
$\times$ speed, the start-up time is the median delay from bind to task
start, and the control-plane delay is the mean delay from admission to
Pod creation plus the median first-wave dispatch latency. A second
variant also keeps a finished task's slot reserved for the
\emph{slot-release latency}: the median time, again measured on the
warm-up only, from a task finishing on a node to the next bind on that
node (2.25--2.75~s across the three profiles), which covers the kubelet's
status update, the watch event, and the next scheduling tick. The twin then runs
the same batches under the same policies and predicts each trial.
Agreement between the twin and the live system is the evidence for RQ6.

\section{Compared variants}

The variants map onto the comparison planned in the original project
description:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Baseline}: \pol{k8s-default} (the default kube-scheduler
      scoring), plus the controls \pol{fcfs} and \pol{random}.
\item \textbf{Workflow-aware}: \pol{eft}, \pol{heft}, \pol{heft-ra},
      and \pol{heft-ra-cap} (DAG ranks, finish-time placement, data and
      image awareness).
\item \textbf{Project history}: \pol{legacy} (historical simulator
      heuristic), \pol{legacy-v1} (the original live scorer), and
      \pol{legacy-tuned} (the same scorer with searched weights).
\item \textbf{Priorities and preemption}: \pol{adaptive} and
      \pol{adaptive-no-preempt}.
\item \textbf{Reference}: \pol{oracle-static}, an exhaustive search over
      static assignments for workflows with few tasks; it is never counted
      as a competitor.
\end{itemize}

Cold-start awareness and gang scheduling are mechanisms present in several
policies rather than separate policies; Section~\ref{sec:mechanisms}
isolates them.

\section{Metrics}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Makespan} of a closed workflow, and \textbf{response time}
      (finish minus arrival) of each workflow in a workload.
\item \textbf{Regret} of a policy on a case: its makespan (or mean
      response) divided by the best deployable policy's on the same case,
      minus one. Reported as mean (with a 95\% bootstrap confidence
      interval~\cite{efron1993bootstrap}), median, and 95th percentile
      (P95), because tail behaviour is what users notice~\cite{dean2013tail}.
\item \textbf{Win/tie rate}: share of cases with zero regret.
\item \textbf{Paired ratio}: for two policies on identical cases, the mean
      of the per-case ratio, with a bootstrap interval and the counts of
      wins, ties, and losses.
\item \textbf{Deadline hit rate}, \textbf{energy}, \textbf{data moved},
      \textbf{preemptions}, \textbf{time-to-running}, and \textbf{cold
      starts} where relevant.
\end{itemize}

\section{Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\paragraph{Four phases.} Seeds are split into an \emph{exploratory} phase
(0--19), used for all development including the design of HEFT-RA; a
\emph{confirmatory} phase (200--249), run once the code was frozen; and a
\emph{holdout} phase (400--449), which was not looked at before every
choice had been made. The capacity-aware lookahead was added afterwards,
in response to the live trials, so the holdout had already been seen when
it was designed; a second, fresh holdout (seeds 600--649) was therefore
run once, for the final policy set, and is the primary evidence below. The
weight search and the contextual search use their own disjoint seeds and
their own train/validation/holdout splits.

\paragraph{Why every seed is perturbed.} The earlier evaluation reported
``20 seeds per case'', but a seed only changes a run through sampled
uncertainty, jitter, and random failures, and four of the five clusters
declare no uncertainty. Counting distinct outcomes over the 20 exploratory
seeds of each (cluster, workflow, policy) showed how few independent
experiments there really were: 1.4 on average for the homogeneous
cluster, 3.0 for the balanced one, 4.0 for the unreliable one and 4.3 for
the edge cluster (only the laptop profile, with 25\% declared uncertainty,
had 20). Intervals computed as if those were 20 independent samples would
have been far too narrow. The confirmatory and both holdout phases
therefore impose an uncertainty floor of 0.1, which turns every seed into a
distinct cluster instance within $\pm10\%$ of the declared one; on the
holdout every one of the 50 seeds gives a distinct outcome for every
cluster, case, and policy. The exploratory phase keeps the declared
values, so comparing phases also shows whether the conclusions survive the
perturbation.

\section{Why HEFT fails: a fan-out case}
\label{sec:heft-failure}

Before the aggregate results, one case explains most of what follows. On
\texttt{balanced-heterogeneous-4}, \texttt{fanout-1to8} has a source that
reads 4~GiB from disk and eight CPU workers that each need a 1~GiB copy
of the source's output (Table~\ref{tab:fanout}).

HEFT puts the source on \texttt{io-fast}, whose 7~GB/s drive finishes the
read at 1.10~s instead of 2.79~s on \texttt{cpu-fast}, a locally correct
choice. It then estimates each worker's finish time on \texttt{cpu-fast}
as if that worker's 1~GiB transfer had the 238~MiB/s link to itself (about
8.7~s), so all eight workers choose \texttt{cpu-fast}. Their eight
transfers then share the link: 8~GiB at 238~MiB/s takes 34.4~s, the workers
start at 37.15~s, and the workflow ends at 40.42~s, six times later than
FCFS. This is the contention-free assumption that Sinnen and
Sousa~\cite{sinnen2005contention} warned about, made concrete. HEFT-RA's
contended estimates alone keep seven workers next to the data (12.94~s);
its lookahead then moves the source itself to \texttt{cpu-fast}, where
every transfer disappears (6.63~s).

Several observations follow.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{HEFT-RA is close to the best policy almost everywhere.} Its
      mean regret is 0.37\% on the fresh holdout, and in 95\% of cases it
      is the best policy or tied with it. The paired ratio against HEFT is
      0.9602 [0.9561, 0.9642] with 712 wins, 3,015 ties and 23 losses
      (first holdout: 0.9604, 701 / 3,023 / 26).
\item \textbf{The capacity-aware lookahead changes nothing for single
      workflows.} \pol{heft-ra-cap} and \pol{heft-ra} make identical
      decisions on all 3,750 cases of each holdout: simulated nodes run 8
      to 16 tasks at once, so a producer's children always find capacity.
\item \textbf{The order is stable across phases.} The confirmatory phase
      gives HEFT-RA 0.38\% mean and 0.00\% P95 regret and HEFT 12.31\% and
      49.91\%; the exploratory phase (declared uncertainty, twelve
      policies) gives HEFT-RA 1.76\% and 5.75\% and HEFT 11.34\% and
      57.20\%.
\item \textbf{HEFT's large regret is mostly a change of reference.} Regret
      is measured against the best policy, which is now HEFT-RA. Without
      HEFT-RA in the set, HEFT's first-holdout regret is 2.44\% mean and
      9.44\% P95, the best of the remaining policies; HEFT remains the best
      of the classic heuristics.
\item \textbf{DAG awareness matters a great deal.} \pol{k8s-default} has
      160.69\% mean regret; the paired ratio HEFT/\pol{k8s-default} is
      0.8133 [0.8039, 0.8230]. FCFS (502\%) and Random (2,811\%) in the
      exploratory phase show how large placement effects are.
\item \textbf{Preemption is irrelevant for a single workflow}:
      \pol{adaptive} and \pol{adaptive-no-preempt} are identical; Adaptive's
      penalties make it worse than plain HEFT (paired ratio 1.0335).
\item \textbf{Tuning the Legacy scorer helps} (\pol{legacy-tuned} 18.34\%
      vs \pol{legacy-v1} 27.80\%; paired ratio 0.9710) but cannot close the
      gap: a weighted sum of normalised factors does not estimate finish
      time.
\item \textbf{Against the static oracle} (300 small exploratory cases),
      HEFT-RA has the smallest mean gap (2.53\%, median 0\%), followed by
      HEFT (3.06\%) and EFT (3.85\%).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Where HEFT-RA loses.} On 17 of the 3,750 first-holdout cases
HEFT-RA is more than 1\% slower than HEFT, and all 17 are
\texttt{unreliable-thermal-3}/\texttt{mixed-etl}. There, the fast node
fails at a scripted time (20~s) that no policy can know. HEFT-RA correctly
predicts that the ETL chain finishes earlier on that node (its aggregate
step ends at 17.5~s instead of 24.5~s), but in some seeds the failure hits
the final export, which must wait for the repair. Over the 50 seeds of
this pair HEFT-RA still wins 39 and loses 11 (mean ratio 0.878; median
33.9~s against 46.1~s). Measured against the best policy of each case,
however, its mean regret in that cell is 18.18\% on the fresh holdout,
where Adaptive and tuned Legacy, which avoid the risky node, tie for the
best. The losses are the price of a risk-neutral estimator. A second,
structural limit is \texttt{homogeneous-4}/\texttt{fanin-8to1}, where
packing beats the list schedulers because consolidating the eight
producers avoids gathering their outputs; fixing it needs group-level
packing~\cite{grandl2014tetris} rather than a better per-task estimate,
and we did not tune for it.

the urgent workflow faster: its median urgent response is 7.6~s against
9.7~s for Adaptive, and per run it is faster more often in every workload
(139 against 83 runs on \texttt{mixed-arrivals}, 99 against 73 on
\texttt{priority-burst}, 138 against 6 on \texttt{recovery-gang-mix}). Its
urgent deadline hit rate is the same or slightly higher (80\% against 80\%,
54\% against 52\%, and 75\% against 71\%); the runs in which only one of
the two meets the 12~s deadline are knife-edge cases, decided by about a
tenth of a second, and 22 of the 24 that favour HEFT-RA are on the
homogeneous cluster. Adaptive's value is the \emph{tail}: when the urgent
workflow arrives behind long batch work, HEFT-RA's rank order lets it wait,
and its P95 urgent response is 126.6~s; Adaptive's priority ordering alone
cuts that to 75.4~s, and preemption to 21.0~s. Priority ordering also has a
price: against HEFT-RA, Adaptive makes the other workflows 7.8\% slower per
run (ratio 1.0781 [1.0519, 1.1097]; 56.9~s against 55.2~s in mean). H5
holds: preemption bounds the urgent workflow's worst case with a negligible
cost relative to the same policy without it, but a priority-first policy
costs typical urgent latency and non-urgent work relative to a
makespan-first one.

\section{Learning, tuning, and portfolios}

\paragraph{Weight search.} The Legacy scorer's weights were searched with
256 seeded log-normal candidates at a fixed absolute scale (scaling all
weights by a constant does not change any decision), selected on 90
training and 45 validation cases, and evaluated once on 75 holdout cases.
After the simulator corrections the search selected the same vector as
before. With warm profiles the tuned/original objective is 0.9759 (mean
makespan 42.18~s to 41.80~s, data moved 434.7 to 272.5~MiB); with cold
profiles it is 0.8601 (mean makespan 99.98~s to 54.89~s). Learning
matters: the original scorer with cold profiles is more than twice as slow
as with warm ones, and tuning helps most exactly when profiles are poor
(H4).

\paragraph{Factor ablation.} Removing one factor at a time on 750 cases
(seeds 300--309) shows that type affinity dominates (without it the
objective is 3.4 times worse), that data locality (+0.088) and load
balance (+0.072) matter, and that resource fit and node availability have
no practical effect. Warm image, failure penalty and memory pressure never
change a decision in the broad corpus, but each one flips the decision in
a controlled stress case, so they are kept.

\paragraph{Contextual portfolio.} A separate search over 160 generated
workflows in 20 (resource mix $\times$ topology) contexts tried to assign a
specialist policy to each context, replacing the default only for a
validation gain of at least 0.5\% with no mean or P95 regression. It kept
HEFT in 19 contexts and chose HEFT-RA for CPU-bound fan-outs. On 600
holdout cases, relative to HEFT (lower is better), HEFT-RA alone scores
0.9952, the portfolio 0.9977, Adaptive 0.9989, and EFT 1.0254: using
HEFT-RA everywhere beats the portfolio, so no portfolio is recommended.

\section{Live validation on Kubernetes}
\label{sec:live}

\paragraph{Protocol.} Each of the three emulated profiles
(Section~\ref{sec:emulation}) was created from scratch, its applied
\texttt{tc} qdiscs, container caps and speed table were recorded, and the
control plane was installed. After a bandwidth-probe round and the
warm-up, 54 trials ran in a seeded random order: two batches, nine live
policies, three repetitions, each trial starting from the same warm
profiles. The \texttt{priority} batch fills all six slots with six long
best-effort tasks and, 7~s later, submits a two-task \texttt{CRITICAL}
workflow and three batch workflows (20 tasks); the \texttt{dag} batch
submits four DAGs at once: a pipeline with a 96~MiB producer, a fan-out
whose producer sends 96~MiB to each of four consumers, a fork-join, and
independent mixed tasks (17 tasks). All 162 trials passed the integrity
gate: every task succeeded on the node its own metrics report, at that
node's emulated speed; every consumer read exactly the bytes its producer
wrote, locally when co-located and remotely otherwise; the observed qdiscs
matched the profile; every trial ran the intended policy with no
scheduler errors; and only Adaptive preempted.

\paragraph{Emulation fidelity.} Calibrated on the warm-up, the
speed-normalised base runtimes were 3.03--3.07~s for the CPU template
(target 3.0~s), 10.02--10.06~s for the long CPU template (10.0~s),
3.01~s for I/O (3.0~s), 3.05--3.25~s for large I/O (3.0~s) and
4.16--4.43~s for memory (4.0~s; allocating the buffer adds a little).
Measured egress was 6.8~MiB/s for the balanced edge worker shaped at
60~Mbit/s (7.15~MiB/s) and 4.5~MiB/s for the edge workers shaped at
40~Mbit/s (4.77~MiB/s).

\paragraph{A defect found by the live runs.} The first complete live run
showed Pods pending for more than 30~s while nodes sat idle, most often
under HEFT-RA. The cause was a race in the scheduler's periodic refresh: the
inventory is polled outside the scheduling lock, so a Pod that finished
between the poll and the merge still looked running, was charged as
``untracked'', and made its node look full until the next refresh
30~seconds later. Locality-seeking policies suffered most, because the
frozen node is exactly the one where a producer's children were meant to
run. The merge now ignores Pods whose completion or preemption the
scheduler has processed (with a regression test that reproduces the
race), the harness now records scheduler-wide errors that carry no Pod
name, and all live results below come from a complete rerun with the fix;
the earlier data is kept only as evidence of the defect. The fix is
visible in the raw timelines: measuring, in every trial without
preemption, the longest period during which a Pod was pending while some
slot was free, the longest such stall was 33--38~s before the fix (13
stalls longer than 10~s in 69 trials) and 10.3~s after it (2 such stalls
in 144 trials). The typical longest stall of 4--6~s per trial is the normal
hand-over of a slot (Section~\ref{sec:twin}) and is the same in both runs.

\paragraph{Concurrent DAGs.} Table~\ref{tab:live-pooled} pools the nine
trials of each policy over the three profiles. The robust live result is
that HEFT-RA was the fastest policy: a HEFT-RA variant had the lowest
\texttt{dag} makespan in 8 of the 9 (profile, repetition) runs, which would
happen with probability of about $4\times10^{-5}$ if the nine policies were
interchangeable; the exception was one edge-profile repetition won by HEFT.
The mechanism is the one seen in simulation. On the balanced profile
HEFT-RA placed the 96~MiB fan-out producer on the CPU-optimised node,
which has both the fastest egress and the fastest CPU for its four CPU
consumers, in all six of its trials, while HEFT left it on the
memory-optimised node in all three; HEFT then spent up to 2.8 times as long
staging inputs (12.2~s against 4.4~s summed over the batch). Paired over matched trials, HEFT-RA's makespan is
0.917 [0.837, 0.998] of HEFT's (8 wins, 1 loss). That interval barely
excludes one, and HEFT's own live result was not stable: on the balanced
profile its median was 39.4~s in the earlier run (which still had the
refresh defect) and 46.0~s in the rerun,
while HEFT-RA's was about 40~s in both, so the 8.3\% figure should be read
as ``HEFT-RA was consistently among the fastest'' rather than as a
precise effect size. The other contrasts are not conclusive with nine

\paragraph{Priorities under contention.} On the \texttt{priority} batch
makespans are within 4\% of each other except for Adaptive, whose
kill-and-restart preemptions (31 over nine trials) raised makespan by
about 14\% (1.139 [0.997, 1.285] against Adaptive without preemption, not
conclusive) and P95 response by 26\% (1.258 [1.054, 1.458]). In return the
critical workflow finishes 12\% sooner (0.878 [0.754, 0.969], 8 wins and
no losses). This batch is the tail case of the simulated priority study:
the critical workflow arrives while long best-effort tasks fill every
slot. The HEFT family orders ready tasks by upward rank alone, so the two
short urgent tasks wait behind higher-ranked batch work: HEFT's critical
response is 1.476 [1.245, 1.746] times \pol{kube-default}'s (0 wins in 9),
whose PrioritySort queue puts them first. In this live batch even
first-come ordering (EFT, 16.56~s) serves the critical workflow almost as
well as priority ordering (Adaptive without preemption, 16.65~s), so most
of the live gap comes from ranking urgent work last, and preemption adds a
further 12\%. The live system therefore confirms the second simulated
conclusion as well: makespan-optimal placement and priority-aware ordering
are different goals, and urgent work arriving into a busy cluster needs
the latter. The capacity-aware lookahead was marginally slower than the
original on this batch (mean response 1.024 [1.001, 1.046], 2 wins and 7
losses), one of 45 contrast-metric pairs computed, so it is reported but
not interpreted.

\section{Answers to the research questions}
\label{sec:answers}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2cm,style=nextline]
\item[RQ1] \textbf{Yes.} In simulation, HEFT's makespan is 0.8133 of the
      default Kubernetes scoring's on the fresh holdout, and HEFT-RA's mean
      regret is 0.37\% against 160.69\%. Live, on four concurrent DAGs, a
      HEFT-RA variant was the fastest of nine policies in 8 of 9 runs and
      its pooled median makespan is 34.0~s against 39.0~s for
      \pol{kube-default}; HEFT-RA is 0.917 [0.837, 0.998] of HEFT's
      makespan, while plain HEFT's advantage over \pol{kube-default}
      (0.930 [0.857, 1.006]) is not conclusive with nine pairs. H1 holds:
      strongly in simulation, and live for the contention-aware variant.
\item[RQ2] \textbf{Yes for aggregate response; urgent work needs a
      bounded tail.} With overlapping workloads the capacity-aware HEFT-RA
      has the lowest median (0.00\%) and tail (63.76\%) response regret and
      the highest deadline hit rate (71.47\% against 36.27\% for
      \pol{k8s-default}), so the single-workflow winner also wins on
      aggregate (H2). For the urgent workflow alone, HEFT-RA is faster in
      the typical case (median 7.6~s against 9.7~s for Adaptive) and meets
      the urgent deadline as often, but its rank order can leave urgent
      work behind long batch tasks: its P95 urgent response is 126.6~s
      against 21.0~s for Adaptive, and live, where the critical workflow
      always arrives into a full cluster, the HEFT family's critical
      response is 1.48 times \pol{kube-default}'s.
\item[RQ3] \textbf{A little, and less than prefetching would.} Image-aware
      estimates lower makespan by 1.2\% and time-to-running by 1.5\%;
      pre-pulling every image would lower them by 7.8\% and about 49\%.
      H3 holds in its weak form.
\item[RQ4] \textbf{Yes.} With cold profiles the original Legacy scorer's
      mean makespan is 99.98~s against 42.18~s with warm profiles, and
      tuning its weights helps most when profiles are cold (objective
      0.8601 against 0.9759 with warm profiles). The live warm-up gives
      every learning policy its profiles before the trials. The tuned
      weights, however, did not transfer to the live cluster (1.043 of
      \pol{legacy-v1}'s makespan on the DAG batch), so weights tuned in
      simulation should be re-validated live. H4 holds for learning, not
      for transferring tuned weights.
\item[RQ5] \textbf{Yes, with a cost that depends on restart price.}
      In simulation preemption is triggered in 100 of 750 runs, roughly
      halves the urgent response when it is, cuts the P95 urgent response
      from 75.4~s to 21.0~s, and costs the other workflows 0.06\%. Live it
      shortens the critical response by 12\% (8 of 9 paired trials), but
      killed tasks restart from scratch, so P95 response grows by 26\% and
      makespan by about 14\% (interval includes no change). H5 holds.
\item[RQ6] \textbf{Partly.} The main simulated finding survives on the
      live control plane: HEFT-RA was the fastest policy for concurrent
      DAGs, for the same reason (it keeps data-heavy producers where their
      consumers can run and pays less for staging), and the live system
      confirms the priority trade-off. The finer ordering of the other
      policies does not transfer reliably: EFT, almost equal to HEFT in
      simulation, was the slowest live policy, and HEFT's own live result
      varied between runs (39.4~s and 46.0~s median on the balanced
      profile). A calibrated twin reproduces live
      times within 3--8\% on the heterogeneous profiles, but only after
      the Kubernetes slot-release latency is modelled, and its ranking of
      policies agrees only moderately with the live one (Spearman
      0.30--0.80). Absolute simulated gains are larger than live gains,
      because the live tasks are short compared with control-plane
      latencies and every node has one or two slots.
\end{description}

\section{Threats to validity}

\paragraph{Construct validity.} Makespan and response regret measure
distance from the best \emph{deployable} policy in our portfolio, not from
the optimum; the static oracle shows that the optimum can be up to 30\%
better on some small cases. Energy and deadlines are reported but were not
the selection criteria.

\paragraph{Internal validity.} The simulator is deterministic for a seed,
policies see identical clusters and identical failures on a seed, and the
holdout data was not consulted during development. Two properties of the
simulated comparison must be kept in mind. First, every policy plans with
the exact parameters of the cluster instance it runs on; only transfer
jitter and the timing of random failures are unknown to it. The perturbed
confirmatory and holdout phases therefore test robustness across cluster
instances, not robustness to wrong estimates. Second, HEFT-RA's estimator
calls the simulator's own resource models (max-min channel sharing, TCP
ceilings, the RC thermal model), and it was designed on exploratory seeds
by the same person who wrote the simulator. Its near-zero simulated regret
is therefore partly by construction and is best read as a best case for a
scheduler whose estimates match reality; HEFT and EFT also plan with exact
parameters, so the simulated gap to them measures what modelling
contention is worth, not what it is worth with noisy estimates. The live
trials, in which every policy works from learned runtimes and measured
bandwidths, are the test of the latter: HEFT-RA remained the fastest
policy there, but its advantage is known far less precisely
(Section~\ref{sec:live}). The earlier
evaluation's defects (Appendix~\ref{app:corrections}) are themselves a
reminder that subtle implementation errors can change conclusions; every
fix has a regression test.

\paragraph{External validity.} The clusters are synthetic, the workflows
are representative structures rather than traces, and the live clusters
share one laptop: their ``nodes'' have no independent thermal or failure
domains, their links are shaped rather than physical, and the host's CPU
is shared through cgroups. The Mathis bound describes loss-based TCP; CUBIC
or BBR would deliver more on long lossy paths. Failures in the simulator
are Poisson or scripted, while real failures are bursty and
correlated~\cite{schroeder2010failures}. Conclusions should therefore be
read as ``under these modelled conditions, and confirmed on a real control
plane with emulated heterogeneity'', not as physical-cluster results.

\paragraph{Statistical validity.} Bootstrap intervals assume cases are
exchangeable; cases share clusters and workflows, so intervals are
somewhat optimistic. Paired win/tie/loss counts, phase-to-phase stability,
and the distinct-outcome check are reported alongside them for that
reason. The live matrix has only three repetitions per profile, batch and
policy (nine pooled trials per policy and batch), and the three
repetitions of a profile share one warm-up, so they are not independent
with respect to calibration noise: HEFT's median on the balanced profile
was 46.0~s in the final run but 39.4~s in the earlier run, even though
that run still had the refresh defect, which could only slow it down. Differences of a few
percent between live policies are therefore within run-to-run noise; only
contrasts whose paired intervals exclude one are interpreted, and, of 45
contrast-metric pairs, a few would cross that line by chance. The
capacity-aware lookahead was designed after the first holdout had been
seen, which is why a second, fresh holdout was run for it.

\chapter{Limitations and Future Directions}
\label{ch:limits}

\paragraph{Risk-neutral estimates.} HEFT-RA minimises expected finish
time. When a fast node fails at an unlucky moment, as in the
\texttt{mixed-etl} case on the unreliable cluster, the expected-value
choice loses. A risk-averse variant could minimise a high quantile of the
finish time or add the variance of the failure delay; it should be
evaluated on the same protocol before adoption.

\paragraph{Priority-aware HEFT-RA.} The HEFT family orders ready tasks by
upward rank only, which serves urgent workflows poorly (live critical
response 1.48 times that of a priority-first queue). Ordering by priority
class first and by rank within a class, while keeping HEFT-RA's placement,
should combine Adaptive's urgent response with HEFT-RA's makespan; it is
the most promising next policy to evaluate.

\paragraph{Group-level packing.} No list scheduler in the portfolio
consolidates a fan-in's producers to avoid gathering their outputs, which
is why FCFS wins \texttt{homogeneous-4}/\texttt{fanin-8to1}. Packing-aware
DAG scheduling in the spirit of Tetris or Graphene is a natural extension.

\paragraph{Admission control.} The live engine orders admission by
priority and arrival but has no quotas or fair sharing between tenants.
Integrating with Kueue, or implementing DRF-style admission, would let the
system protect throughput and fairness under sustained overload.

\paragraph{Scheduler integration.} The scheduler is a separate process
selected through \texttt{schedulerName}. A production implementation
would be a plugin of the Kubernetes scheduling framework (Score, Permit for
gangs, PostFilter for preemption) and could use the Workload API for
gangs.

\paragraph{Live fidelity.} Thermal behaviour and failures were not
emulated live (all Kind nodes share one CPU package and one kernel), so
HEFT-RA's thermal and failure terms were validated only in simulation.
Links are shaped only on egress; congestion inside the shared Docker
bridge is not modelled by the twin. A physical cluster running
k3s~\cite{k3s} (scripts are provided) is the next validation step.

\paragraph{Learning.} Profiles learn runtimes per template and node; they
do not yet predict runtimes from input size or features, and drift
detection only flags divergence. Learned runtime models and per-workflow
adaptation remain future work, as does a learned scheduler evaluated with
the same holdout discipline.

\paragraph{Multi-objective and multi-cluster.} Energy, cost and fairness
are measured but not optimised jointly, and scheduling across clusters or
along an edge-cloud continuum is out of scope.

\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{ch:conclusion}

This thesis set out to find out whether a workflow-aware scheduler can
place the tasks of DAG workflows on heterogeneous clusters better than the
default, Pod-by-Pod behaviour of Kubernetes, and which algorithm to use
for which kind of workflow and cluster.

The first result is methodological. An audit of the earlier version of the
project found that several of its conclusions rested on defects: a lower
bound that was not a bound, random failures that depended on the policy,
seeds that mostly repeated the same experiment, links that were half
duplex for every medium, and a live emulation in which every node ran
tasks at the same speed. A second, live-only defect, a refresh race that
froze nodes for 30~seconds, was found only by running the full live
matrix. None of these errors was visible in aggregate tables; each was
found by following one concrete trace. The corrected simulator,
three-phase protocol with a fresh holdout, emulated live clusters, and
calibrated twin are the infrastructure on which the other results rest.

The second result is an algorithm. HEFT, the standard heterogeneous list
scheduler, fails badly whenever many transfers share a link, because its
estimates assume every transfer has the link to itself; in the clearest
case it is six times slower than FCFS. HEFT-RA keeps HEFT's order and
fixes the estimate (contended transfers, shared image pulls, thermal
prediction, expected failure loss) and adds a capacity-aware one-level
lookahead. It has no tuned weights. On 3,750 fresh holdout cases its mean
makespan regret is 0.37\% against 12.42\% for HEFT and 160.69\% for the
default scoring, it leads every workflow and cluster family, and with
overlapping workloads it leads median and tail response and deadline
hits.

The third result is that the main simulated finding survives on a real
control plane. On emulated heterogeneous Kind clusters, a HEFT-RA variant
was the fastest of nine policies for four concurrent DAGs in 8 of 9 runs,
for the same reason as in simulation: it kept the data-heavy producer
where its consumers could run and spent less time staging inputs. Its
makespan was 0.917 of HEFT's, although HEFT's own live result varied
between runs and the finer ordering of the other policies did not
transfer. The live system also confirmed the main trade-off: urgent work
arriving into a busy cluster needs priority-first ordering, and
preemption buys about 12\% faster critical response at the price of
restarted work. The twin, once it modelled the measured slot-release
latency of Kubernetes, reproduced live times within 3--8\% on the
heterogeneous profiles, which supports using the simulator for
comparative questions while reminding that control-plane latencies must
be part of the model.

The recommendation is therefore: use HEFT-RA (with the capacity-aware
lookahead) for DAG workflows on heterogeneous clusters, especially when
data moves between tasks or the network is uneven; it also serves urgent
work well in the typical case. Where an urgent workflow may arrive while
long batch work fills the cluster and its worst-case latency matters, use
a priority-first policy such as Adaptive, with preemption only where
restarts are cheap: it cuts the P95 urgent response from about two minutes
to about twenty seconds in simulation. Prefer risk-averse placement on
clusters whose fastest nodes are also the least reliable, and add image
pre-pulling where start-up latency matters, because placement alone
recovers little of it. The default Kubernetes scoring should not be used
to place DAG workflows, although its priority-first queue is worth keeping.
Physical multi-machine validation, a priority-aware and risk-averse
HEFT-RA, and a scheduling-framework implementation are the natural next
steps.
